Part I · What makes a known word unrecognisable — Chapter 01

Words you cannot look up

The longest word in the letter is not the hardest one. It is usually three or four words you already know, written without spaces, and it comes apart in a fixed direction.

The device Dutch joins nouns into single written words, with no limit on how many. Many of the results will never appear as dictionary entries. In an ordinary noun compound the rightmost element is the head — it tells you what kind of thing the word denotes — and everything to its left narrows it.

Step 1 On the page

Two sentences in the register of a letter from the Belastingdienst:

In the style of an assessment letter

Voor de berekening van de heffingskortingen gebruiken wij uw inkomensgegevens over het belastingjaar.

De premie voor uw arbeidsongeschiktheidsverzekering staat vermeld op de jaaropgave van uw verzekeraar.

Where the eye stops is predictable. It is not voor, van or uw, which you have known for years. It is heffingskortingen, inkomensgegevens, belastingjaar, arbeidsongeschiktheidsverzekering and jaaropgave — five words, one of them thirty-four letters long. Even where a dictionary lists them, it gives you nothing for the next compound, and Dutch will keep making new ones.

The usual response is to treat a long word as a single unknown item, copy it into a translator, and accept whatever comes back without being able to check it. That works, in the sense that a taxi works when you cannot read the map. This chapter is the map.

Step 2 What it is doing

The rule

Dutch forms compounds productively: any noun can be stuck to the front of another noun to make a new one, and the result is written as one word. Because the process is productive and unbounded, a great many compounds are made as needed and never enter a dictionary at all. Arbeidsongeschiktheidsverzekering is a real, ordinary, bureaucratic word; it is also not a word anyone learned. It was assembled.

So it can be disassembled, and the direction matters. Read the ending first. The rightmost element is the head: it says what category of thing you are looking at. Everything before it is modification, applied from right to left.

belastingjaar belastingjaar The head is jaar, a year. Which year? A tax one. So: a tax year — not a kind of tax.

That last line is the whole chapter in miniature. A reader who works left to right meets belasting, recognises "tax", and carries "tax" forward as the subject of the sentence. But the word does not denote a tax. It denotes a year. The first element only tells you which one.

The linking letters

Compounds are often joined with a linking -s- or -en-. Do not read them as plural or possessive markers here: for the purpose of splitting the compound, treat them as linking elements and set them aside. (The spelling of the -en- is historically tied to the plural of the first element, which is why it looks like one. It is not doing that work inside the compound.)

heffingskorting heffingskorting A korting — a reduction. Of what sort? One that applies to a heffing, a levy. The -s- is glue.

Now the long one. Nothing changes except the number of pieces:

arbeidsongeschiktheidsverzekering arbeidsongeschiktheidsverzekering An verzekering — an insurance. Against what? Ongeschiktheid, unfitness. Unfitness for what? Arbeid, labour. Insurance against being unfit to work.

Thirty-four letters, three elements, two pieces of glue, and no step in it is difficult. The difficulty was entirely in not knowing which end to start from.

A pair of hyphens that go missing

One variation is worth knowing before you meet it, because it looks like a typing error. Dutch can suspend the shared half of two compounds and mark the gap with a hanging hyphen:

wet- en regelgeving

That is not wet followed by something unrelated. It is wetgeving en regelgeving — legislation and regulation — with the repeated -geving borrowed from the word on the right. Wherever you see a hyphen dangling before en, look to the end of the next word and read it back onto the first.

What this buys you later

A compound takes its gender from its last element. Donaldson states this as a rule with a listed set of exceptions: het ontbijt and de tafel give de ontbijttafel. So splitting a word does not only tell you what it means — it tells you whether it takes de or het, which is what chapter 05 is built on. Het jaar gives het belastingjaar; de korting gives de heffingskorting. You get that for free, from a skill you needed anyway.

Step 3 Read it

Go back to the second sentence and take the two remaining words.

jaaropgave jaaropgave An opgave — a statement or declaration. Covering what period? A year. An annual statement.
inkomensgegevens inkomensgegevens Gegevens — data, details. About what? Inkomen, income. Income details.

The wrong parse

There is one predictable failure, and it is worth seeing written down, because it does not feel like an error while you are making it.

The parse you will reach for

inkomstenbelasting

First element recognised — inkomsten, income. Conclusion: the sentence is about income. Reading continues.

What it says

inkomsten + belasting

Head on the right: a belasting, a tax. The income part only says which tax. The sentence is about a tax.

The two readings differ by one noun, and in an assessment letter that one noun is the difference between a figure describing what you earned and a figure describing what you owe. The habit to build is small: find the end of the word before you decide what it is.

Three to try

Split these before opening the answers. Each is a genuine term from Dutch tax and insurance correspondence, and each follows the rule from step 2. Write down the head and what you think the whole word names — writing it down is what makes the comparison afterwards worth anything.

  • ziektekostenverzekering
  • rendementsgrondslag
  • volksverzekeringen
Show the worked splits

Heads first: a verzekering, a grondslag (a basis), and verzekeringen again. Then the modifiers, right to left: insurance for the costs of illness; the basis on which a return is calculated; and insurance covering the population — the Dutch national insurance schemes.

Two things to notice in your own answers. The third has a linking -s- that is easy to mistake for part of volks. And grondslag is itself an old compound whose parts no longer help — some compounds have set into single lexical items, and those you do simply learn. That second one is not a detail; it is the limit of the whole method, and the next box is about it.

Did it work?

Nobody has an answer key for the letter on your table. These are the tests that run without one — most can only tell you that you are wrong, which is still the thing worth knowing.

  • Reassembly. Write your parts out and join them back up, putting any linking letters back. The result must spell the original word exactly. A letter left over, or a letter missing, means the split is wrong — not approximately wrong, wrong.
  • Two parts, minimum. If one of your "parts" is the whole word with the rest tacked on, you have not decomposed anything. A split with one lexical constituent is not a split.
  • The kind test. The compound should name a kind of its head. A ziektekostenverzekering is a kind of verzekering, not a kind of ziekte. If your reading makes it a kind of the first element, you have read the word from the wrong end.
  • The linking-element test. A linking -s- or -en- belongs to neither part. Delete it: the left-hand part must still be a word on its own. In huisarts deleting the s leaves hui, so that s was never a link — it is part of huis.
  • The sentence test. If the head does not name the kind of thing the sentence is actually about, either you split in the wrong place or the word is lexicalised. Try the split again first; reach for lexicalisation second.

Where this procedure cannot decide

Splitting tells you the category of a compound. It does not tell you the meaning, and it cannot tell you when it has stopped telling you the meaning.

grondslag decomposes cleanly into grond and slag — ground, and a blow or stroke. Every test above passes. The parts reassemble, there are two of them, the head is on the right, and the word does name a kind of thing. It just is not a kind of slag: the word means a basis, and the parts have nothing to do with it.

There is no signal in the word itself that this has happened. A lexicalised compound looks exactly like a transparent one, which means the procedure can hand you a confident, well-formed, wrong answer — and the only thing that catches it is the sentence refusing to make sense. When that happens, stop splitting and look the whole word up. Knowing that you have hit this case is the skill; forcing the parts to mean something is how a reader ends up further from the letter than when they started.

Step 4 In your own post

This week, take one letter that is already in the house — an assessment, an insurance renewal, a statement from a pension fund — and do only this: find the three longest words on the page and split each one. Do not translate the sentence. Do not look anything up until you have written down what you think the head element is.

Two places repay it most. The subject line of an official letter is often a single compound doing the work of a sentence, and it tells you what the letter is for before you read a word of the body. And the row labels in any table of figures are almost entirely compounds; splitting them turns an opaque column of numbers into a list of statements about specific things.

Run the same five tests from step 3 on each split — reassembly first, because it is the one that fails loudest. You are not checking whether your translation is good; you are checking whether your decomposition is possible.

Then write down, for each word, which of three things you have: a split you are confident in, a split that reassembles but whose meaning does not fit the sentence, or a word you could not get into parts at all. The second and third are results, not failures. A word in the second pile is very likely lexicalised and belongs in a dictionary lookup; a word in the third is probably not a compound. Recording which pile a word landed in is what turns this from an exercise you did once into a list you can act on, and it keeps you honest about how much of the letter the method actually opened.

References

The gender rule used above is Donaldson, Dutch Reference Grammar, §7.1.3 (printed page 32), which states that compound nouns take the gender of the last noun and gives the exceptions explicitly. His gender rules generally are §7.1, beginning at printed page 27, where he also notes plainly that many nouns do not fit any rule and must be learned individually — a caveat chapter 05 takes seriously.

Neither De Opmaat nor Taaltalent 1 or 2 treats compound decomposition as a reading technique; their contents pages carry no section for it. The rule above is standard and uncontroversial. What is missing from the teaching material is the instruction to use it while reading.

  • The rightmost element is the head; modification runs right to left
  • Linking -s- and -en- carry no meaning
