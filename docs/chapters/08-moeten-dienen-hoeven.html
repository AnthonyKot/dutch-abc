Part III · Who must do what — Chapter 08

Moeten, dienen, hoeven

You learned moeten in your first month. Official writing often states the same requirement with a verb that means to serve — and the sentence telling you that you need do nothing is built from a third verb that looks related to neither.

Before you start (ch. 07)

You find er with a stranded preposition, and nothing before it can be what it stands for. What do you do?

Answer

Look forward, for a dat-clause or an om … te-clause. The pointer may be holding a place open for content that has not arrived yet. Only with no referent in either direction is it one of the other uses of er.

The device Dutch has one everyday verb for obligation and a different one for writing it down. Spoken Dutch says u moet; a letter says u dient te. The two are not stylistic variants to a reader — they behave differently in the sentence, and the difference is a single small word. And negation does not simply reverse an obligation: it switches to a third verb, and which verb carries the negative decides whether you are being released or forbidden.

Step 1 On the page

Three sentences of the kind that decide whether a letter needs answering.

Betreft: uw aanvraag

Het ingevulde formulier dient vóór 1 juni bij de gemeente te worden ingeleverd.

Bent u al ingeschreven, dan hoeft u geen bewijs van inschrijving mee te sturen.

Kunt u het bedrag niet in één keer betalen, dan kunt u een betalingsregeling aanvragen.

All three decide whether this letter requires action from you. Only the first actually imposes an obligation — and none of the three contains moeten.

The first stops the eye at dient. A dictionary gives dienen as to serve, which produces a sentence about a form serving something — and then the sentence goes on for eight more words and ends in a participle you now recognise from chapter 04. The second uses hoeft, which many readers have never met at all: it is not in the first thousand words, and it appears in a beginner's course, if at all, as a footnote to moeten.

The third is the dangerous one, because it is easy. Kunt u — can you — reads as a question about ability, and a reader who has been told all year that kunnen means can will take it that way. It is not a question. Both halves of that sentence are doing something else, and they are not doing the same thing as each other.

These are the sentences a reader most needs and is least equipped for. Moeten has not gone anywhere — it is good Dutch in every register, and it appears in official letters too. But official writing frequently uses dienen te where everyday Dutch would use moeten, and a reader who knows only moeten can therefore read straight past an explicit requirement. That is the difference between a letter you must act on this week and one you can file.

Step 2 What it is doing

The written form of moeten

In formal Dutch, dienen replaces moeten in the sense to be obliged to. It is not a softer word or a politer one. It is the same instruction in the register a public body writes in. Both verbs are correct and both occur in official letters; dienen te is the characteristically bureaucratic choice.

U moet het formulier opsturen. · U dient het formulier op te sturen. The same requirement. The first is what someone would say to you; the second is what the letter prints.

The tell is one word long, and it is te. Modal verbs take a bare infinitive: u moet betalen, with nothing between them. Dienen is not a modal, and whenever it governs an infinitive it takes te: u dient te betalen. So the small word you have been reading past is the thing that tells you which construction you are in.

Where the te sits is not fixed, and it is worth being exact about this. It does not simply precede the last word of the clause. It sits immediately before the infinitive it belongs to, and that infinitive is often not the final one.

u dient te betalen One infinitive; te is in front of it and it is also the last word. The simple case.
dient … te worden ingeleverd te belongs to worden, and the final word is a participle two places later. Step 1's first sentence is this shape.
dient … in te dienen A separable verb, so te goes inside it, between particle and stem — chapter 03's split, seen from a new angle.

So the reliable instruction is not look one word back from the end. It is: find the group of verbs the finite verb governs, and look for a te anywhere inside it. Formal Dutch will also write dient afgesloten te worden, with the participle first — same group, same te, different order.

One qualification. Dienen and hoeven take te when they govern an infinitive. Used on their own, with the verb left implied, they take nothing: dat hoeft niet — that is not necessary — is a complete sentence.

VerbTakes te?Reads asRegister
moetennomust, have tospoken, and plain writing

One more member, less common but worth recognising: behoren te and horen te do the same job as dienen te, and also always take te.

Negation does not reverse an obligation. It changes the verb.

This is the part with consequences. Moeten carries two meanings that English also runs together — to be obliged to and to be advised to — and in the affirmative Dutch does not separate them either. But the moment a negative appears, the two meanings go to different verbs.

Released

U hoeft niets te doen.

You need do nothing. The obligation does not apply to you. File the letter.

Forbidden

U moet dit formulier niet gebruiken.

You must not use this form. An instruction, and ignoring it has consequences.

Both sentences are negative and both are about what you do not do, and they are opposite in the only sense that matters. The distinction is not carried by niet. It is carried by which verb the letter chose. Hoeven releases you; moeten with a negative forbids you.

Hoeven is worth a moment on its own, because it is structurally odd. It appears only where something negative or limiting is present — niet, geen, niets, nooit, alleen, pas. You will not meet a plain affirmative u hoeft te betalen; Dutch says u moet betalen. So the verb itself is a signal: seeing hoeven tells you a restriction is somewhere in the sentence, and finding it is the whole job.

Kunt u is four things, and only one is a question

The third sentence in step 1 used kunt twice with two different jobs, which is ordinary rather than perverse.

ReadingExampleWhat it means for you
AbilityKunt u het bedrag niet betalen…a condition being described
Availability…dan kunt u een regeling aanvragenan option you may take
PossibilityDit kan tot vertraging leidena warning, not an instruction
Polite requestKunt u ons uw nieuwe adres doorgeven?an instruction in disguise

The last row is the one to be careful with. A question mark and kunt u can be a genuine request that you do something, and an institution asking whether you are able to supply your address is not what is happening. But none of the four is an obligation, and none of them should be recorded as one. If a letter wants to require something it has moeten and dienen te available, and using neither is itself information.

Step 3 Read it

The procedure

  1. Bracket the clause — finite verb early, the rest at the end (ch. 02).
  2. Name the finite verb. Is it a form of moeten, dienen, hoeven, behoren or kunnen?
  3. Find the verb group the finite verb governs, and look for te anywhere inside it. Present means dienen-type; absent means modal. Do not look only at the word before the last one — te worden ingeleverd and in te dienen put it in different places.
  4. Find the negative, and see which verb it belongs to. Hoeven + negative releases you. Moeten + negative forbids you.
  5. Record the status first, then three things. Status: required / forbidden / not required / optional / requested. Then actor, action, deadline. Any of the three may be missing, and a missing one is a finding, not a gap in your reading.

Applied to step 1's first sentence. The bracket runs from dient to ingeleverd. The finite verb is dienen, and te sits before the final verb group, so this is an obligation in official register. There is no negative. Now the three:

From the sentence
Statusrequired
Actornot stated — the form is the subject, and nobody is named as having to do it
Actionthe completed form is handed in at the gemeente
Deadlinevóór 1 juni

Two of three, and the missing one is the most important. The sentence requires something and does not say of whom. That is not sloppy writing; it is the standard construction of official Dutch, and chapter 09 is about how it is done and how to put the actor back. For now, record the gap and move on — a letter with an unattributed obligation is one to read twice.

You can stop here and use the procedure. Five steps, one small word to look for, and four things to write down. Everything below is the two ways it goes wrong, the case it cannot settle, and where these sentences sit in a real letter.

Three to try

For each, name the verb, find the te or its absence, and write down actor, action and deadline before opening the answers. One of the three is not an obligation at all, and one contains a dienen that is not this chapter's verb.

  1. U dient het bezwaarschrift binnen zes weken na de datum van deze beschikking in te dienen.
  2. Als u het niet eens bent met dit besluit, kunt u bezwaar maken. U hoeft daarvoor geen kosten te betalen.
  3. Stukken die kunnen dienen ter onderbouwing van uw bezwaar, stuurt u mee als bijlage.
Show the worked answers

One. Dient with te nine words later, so an obligation in official register. The final verb is in te dienen — and notice what that is: the separable verb indienen from chapter 03, split around the te, which is exactly where te goes with a separable verb. So the sentence contains dienen and indienen, which are unrelated verbs that look alike. Status: required. Actor: u. Action: submit the notice of objection. Deadline: within six weeks of the date of the decision — a relative deadline, which means you also need the date at the top of the letter to make it real.

Two. Two sentences, and neither is an obligation. Kunt u bezwaar maken is the availability reading from step 2 — an option, not a requirement. Then hoeft with geen and te betalen: you are released, there is no fee. Status: optional for the first sentence, not required for the second. Actor: u. Action: none required. Deadline: none. This is a letter you can file, and knowing that with confidence is worth as much as spotting a deadline.

Three. Dienen appears, and it is not this chapter's verb. Two things prove it. There is no te and no infinitive after it — instead ter onderbouwing van uw bezwaar, an ordinary prepositional phrase. And the sense fits: documents that can serve to support your objection. This is dienen in its dictionary meaning, to serve, with kunnen as the modal in front of it. The obligation in this sentence is carried by something else entirely — stuurt u mee, a plain present tense doing the work of an instruction, with the separable verb meesturen split across the clause.

Did it work?

You have named a verb and written down actor, action and deadline. Five tests, and the first three can each tell you outright that the reading is wrong.

  • The te must match the verb. Moeten and kunnen never take it; dienen, hoeven and behoren always do when they govern an infinitive — used alone, as in dat hoeft niet, they take nothing. Search the whole verb group, not the word before the last. If you have read dient as an obligation and there is no te anywhere in the clause, you have the wrong verb — see the next test.
  • Dienen is also an ordinary verb meaning to serve. The obligation reading requires a te plus an infinitive. If what follows is a noun phrase or a preposition — ter onderbouwing, als bewijs — it is the lexical verb and there is no obligation in the sentence at all.
  • Hoeven needs a negative or a limiter. If you have read a verb as hoeven and nothing in the clause is negative or limiting — niet, geen, niets, nooit, but also alleen, pas, slechts, nauwelijks, zelden — look again. Affirmative obligation is moeten.
  • The negative belongs to a verb, and you must say which. Write it out: released or forbidden. If you cannot decide which of the two a negative sentence is doing, you have not finished, and the difference is the whole content of the sentence.
  • An option is not an obligation — but do not therefore discard it. If your verb was kunnen, the Status line reads optional, or requested where the sentence is a polite request with a question mark. Neither is a legal requirement. Both can still be the sentence you have to answer, and a letter asking whether you can supply your new address is one that needs a reply. Recording it as requested keeps it on the list; recording it as "not an obligation" and stopping there is how actionable post gets filed.

Where this procedure cannot decide

Affirmative moeten means both you are obliged to and you are advised to, and Dutch does not separate them in the affirmative any more than English separates the two senses of you must.

U moet uw gegevens vóór 1 juli doorgeven.

Requirement, with a consequence if you do not? Or strong advice, with nothing behind it? The sentence is identical either way. In a letter about a benefit or a permit that is the difference between a deadline and a suggestion.

Only negation splits them, and letters overwhelmingly use the affirmative — which is precisely why the distinction is invisible where it matters most. The one place Dutch does mark it is register: dient u te is essentially always the obligation sense, because a body writing in that register is stating a requirement rather than offering advice. A plain u moet in an otherwise formal letter is therefore worth a second look — it may be the softer reading.

What actually settles it is not grammar but what sits nearby. Look for a stated consequence — anders, indien niet, a fine, a rejection — or a legal basis, op grond van, volgens artikel. Either turns advice into a requirement. Neither present, and the honest record is requirement or advice, not determined. Chapter 10 is about those connectives and chapter 13 about where in a letter they sit.

Step 4 In your own post

This chapter has a four-line output, and it is the one to build a habit on. For every obligation-shaped sentence, write down status — required, forbidden, not required, optional or requested — and then actor, action, deadline, leaving a line blank where the letter does not supply it. Four lines per letter is the whole exercise, and a stack of them is a to-do list you could not previously make.

The status line is what stops the method throwing away work. Not required and requested both come out of sentences with no obligation in them, and they belong in opposite piles: the first is a letter you can file, the second is one that still wants an answer.

The search that finds them is short. Go through the letter for dient, dienen, moet, hoeft and kunt, and check each one for a te. In most official letters that is under a dozen hits, and it will find the requirements, releases and options that are marked by a verb.

It will not find all of them, and this chapter has already shown you why. Exercise three's instruction was stuurt u mee — a plain present tense, no modal anywhere, doing the work of a command. Official and legal Dutch use the ordinary indicative for obligation routinely. So after the verb search, read once more for imperatives and for plain present-tense sentences addressed to u: U stuurt het formulier mee, Vul het formulier volledig in. Those carry no signal at all except that the sentence is about you and about an action.

Where they cluster is predictable. Forms and their covering instructions are almost pure dienen te — the IND's own garantstelling form, a single sheet of instructions, uses dienen five times, with one hoeft and one plain moet u. And the paragraph after a decision is where objection deadlines live, which is where a missed binnen zes weken costs the most.

Run the same five tests from step 3 on every hit. The second one especially — dienen meaning to serve is common in exactly the documents that are full of dienen te, and the two sit paragraphs apart in the same letter.

Then be explicit about what is unresolved, and there will be two kinds. An affirmative u moet with no consequence and no legal basis stated: record it as requirement or advice, not determined. And any obligation with the Actor line blank — which will be most of them. That blank is not your failure and it is not incidental to the register; it is the subject of the next chapter, and the list you are building now is what chapter 09 will teach you to complete.

References

Donaldson treats the modal auxiliaries at printed pages 146–153. Three sections carry this chapter. §11.9 (printed 146) sets out the class — four true modals, kunnen, moeten, mogen and willen, with hoeven, durven and zullen sharing some of their characteristics — and states the feature this chapter's central test rests on: a modal takes no te before the infinitive it governs.

§11.9.5 (printed 151) gives the two meanings of moeten and the fact that negating them requires two different verbs, with hoeven described as a semi-modal that must sometimes take te — an unmodal trait, in his phrase, and the reason the test works. §11.9.5.2c (printed 152) is the one that answers a question chapter 02 left open: in formal style dienen and (be)horen replace moeten in the sense to be obliged to, and they are always followed by te. The polite-request use of kunnen is §11.9.5.1b, printed 151.

The frequency claim above is a count taken from the IND garantstelling form held in this project's sources: five occurrences of dienen, one hoeft, one moet u. That is a fact about the document; none of its text is reproduced here, and every Dutch sentence in this chapter was written for it.

Taaltalent 2 teaches moeten against hoeven, so a reader who finished it has met the negation rule. Neither course teaches dienen te — and for a reader that is the more serious omission, because it is a form the documents routinely use. The reason is the same one as in chapter 07: both books build toward speaking, and u dient te is primarily formal written language, uncommon in ordinary conversation.

One caution about the age of the grammar. Donaldson also records a first-person moge used to open formal letters, which is genuinely archaic now and which you will not meet in post that arrives this year. His account of dienen te is a different matter and remains exactly right: the IND form quoted from above was printed in this century.

Kaart

Nieuw

  • dienen te is the written form of moeten — same requirement, official register
  • behoren te / horen te do the same job
  • The test is te: modals never take it; dienen and hoeven always do when they govern an infinitive. It sits before the infinitive it belongs to — anywhere in the verb group, not one word from the end
  • hoeven releases you; moeten + negative forbids you. The negative does not decide — the verb does
  • hoeven appears only with niet, geen, niets, alleen, pas
  • dienen is also the ordinary verb to serve — no te, no obligation
  • kunt u is ability, availability, possibility or a polite request — never an obligation
  • Status — required / forbidden / not required / optional / requested — then actor / action / deadline. A blank line is a finding, and requested is not the same as not required

Terug

  • Where does the finite verb sit, and where does the infinitive it governs end up? (ch. 02)

    The finite verb second, the infinitive at the end of the clause. In this chapter that gap is where te hides, one word before the final verb.

  • Why is in te dienen written as three words? (ch. 03)

    Because indienen is separable, and te goes inside a separable verb, between the particle and the stem. That is also how you tell a separable verb from an inseparable one when te is present.

  • What does a participle at the end of a clause need earlier in that clause? (ch. 04)

    An auxiliary governing it — a form of hebben, zijn or worden. Te worden ingeleverd is where chapter 09 begins.

  • A form can be read two ways and the sentence gives no evidence either way. What do you write down? (ch. 03, 07)

    Both readings, marked unresolved. Choosing by instinct produces a confident answer you cannot audit — and here the two readings are "I must" and "I am advised to".
