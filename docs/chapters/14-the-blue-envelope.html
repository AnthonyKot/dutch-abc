Part V · The real documents — Chapter 14

The blue envelope

One letter, read from the top. Every device in this book appears in it and nothing new is introduced — which means that if you get stuck, the chapter that unsticks you has already been written.

Before you start (ch. 13)

You have a letter in front of you and thirty seconds. Which four things do you look at, and in what order?

Answer

The identification block — what this is and what number to quote. The date, because every relative period is counted from something. Then the headings alone, top to bottom, looking for the ones that name an activity or ask a question. Then the action passage inside them, and if the headings gave you nothing, the whole body. The output is chapter 08's line: status, actor, action, deadline, consequence.

No new device This chapter introduces nothing. It is the book being spent: a complete letter of the kind that arrives in a blue envelope, read the way the last thirteen chapters have been preparing you to read it. Every construction in it belongs to a chapter you have already worked through, and the worked reading names which one each time. If you meet something the book never gave you, that is a defect in the book and not in the letter.
The letter is a reconstruction. It was composed for this book, modelled on a real assessment, with invented names, invented figures and placeholder identifiers. No real letter is reproduced anywhere in this book. That is a privacy rule, and it also makes for a better exercise: a real letter uses whichever devices it happens to use, while this one was built to carry all of them.

Step 1 On the page

Read it once, at your own pace, before anything below. Then answer the only two questions that matter: what does it require of you, and by when?

Thirteen chapters ago this was a wall. It is worth being precise about what has changed, because it is not that the words got easier. The vocabulary in this letter is genuinely difficult — verschuldigde, dagtekening, heffingskorting, machtiging — and you were not taught most of it.

What changed is that the letter now has parts. You can see where the metadata stops and the body starts, which sections are headed, which sentence carries an obligation and which carries a condition. A word you do not know sits inside a structure you do, and that is a completely different problem from a wall of text.

Step 2 What it is doing

The whole method, on one page

Thirteen chapters, one line each, in the order you would actually use them. This page is the book.

The order is not the chapter order, and that is the point. The book was arranged so each chapter could be built on the one before it. Reading is arranged the other way round: you start from the largest unit you can see and go down only as far as the obstruction. Many sentences never need chapters 04 to 07 at all.

Two things worth carrying that are not procedures

Grade what you know. Chapter 09 introduced it for actors and chapter 12 extended it to notation, and it applies to everything: stated is what the document says, recoverable is what it implies, probable is what convention suggests, not stated is a real and respectable answer. A reading with grades on it is worth more than a reading without, because you know which parts to check.

And the limit that keeps the grading honest. Probable is not the same as safe to rely on. What a document most likely means and what you should do about it are two questions, and this book answers only the first.

Step 3 Read it

Four sentences to work

Take these four from step 1 and give, for each, the parse and the chapter that supplies it. Write your answers down first. Then run the whole letter for status, actor, action, deadline and consequence before you open the reveal.

  1. Deze definitieve aanslag is vastgesteld op basis van de door u ingediende aangifte.
  2. Wij schrijven het verschuldigde bedrag binnen drie weken na de dagtekening van deze beschikking van uw rekening af, mits u ons daarvoor gemachtigd hebt.
  3. Bij een adreswijziging dient u dit binnen vier weken aan ons door te geven.
  4. Maakt u schriftelijk bezwaar, dan dient uw bezwaarschrift op 28 mei 2026 binnen te zijn.
Show the worked reading

One. Is vastgesteld is a passive with no actor — chapter 09 — and vastgesteld is a separable weak participle, vaststellen, with ge- inside the split (chapters 03 and 04). Then the hard part: de door u ingediende aangifte is an article followed by material no ordinary adjective could begin, so the head is the first noun after the participle — aangifte, chapter 06. The whole phrase is the return submitted by you. Actor: recoverable. The tax office assessed it; the letter does not say so.

Two. The longest sentence in the letter, and it is chapter 02's. Go to the end of the clause first and you find af — a stranded particle, thirteen tokens from schrijven. Joined, afschrijven, to debit. That is chapter 03, and everything between the two halves is when and from where. Then mits — chapter 10's condition that switches the action on — and daarvoor, chapter 07, pointing back to the debit. The sentence says: we will take it, provided you have authorised us to. If you have not, this sentence does nothing at all, which is what the next one is for.

Three. Dient u … door te geven: chapter 08's written obligation, with te wedged inside the separable verb doorgeven — chapter 03 and chapter 08 arriving in the same three words. Adreswijziging splits as adres + wijziging, chapter 01, and the head is the second element. Status: required. Actor: you, stated. Deadline: four weeks, from a change of address rather than from this letter. That last point matters — it is the only period in the letter counted from something other than the letter itself.

Four. A fronted condition with inversion, so the clause reads if you object in writing — chapters 02 and 10. Dient … te zijn is chapter 08 again, and binnen here is the particle of binnen zijn, to have arrived, not the preposition within. And this is the trap chapter 13 warned about. The online route says verstuur … uiterlijk op die datum — send by. This one says the objection must have arrived. Same date, two different requirements, and which one binds you depends on a route you choose.

The whole letter, on chapter 08's line

What it isa final income-tax assessment for 2025, requiring payment
Quote backthe aanslagnummer
Letter date16 April 2026
Statusrequired — but see below, because there are two different requirements and one option
Action 1aif an authorisation exists: nothing — they debit it
Deadline 1athree weeks from 16 April — their window, not yours
Action 1bif no authorisation exists: transfer € 1.245,- yourself, quoting the reference
Deadline 1bnot stated — and this is the sharpest thing in the letter
Action 2notify a change of address — conditional, and only if your address changes
Optionobject, t/m 28 May 2026 — optional, and 28 May is included
Consequencefor the address: post goes to the old one — stated. For non-payment: not stated anywhere in this letter

Look at 1a against 1b before anything else. The two branches of the payment section put the reader in completely different positions, and the letter marks the difference with one word — mits. With an authorisation, nothing is required of you and the three weeks are the tax office's window. Without one, you must transfer the money and the letter states no deadline for doing it. A reader who takes "three weeks" away as their own deadline has read the wrong branch; a reader who takes it away as a deadline at all may be in the branch where none was given.

And the consequence line is the other instructive one. The letter requires € 1.245,- and does not say what happens if you do not pay. Chapter 10 taught that a stated consequence is what settles whether a requirement is real; here there is none, and the requirement is real anyway — because it is an assessment, and that is a fact about the genre rather than about the sentence. Recording consequence: not stated is the correct output, and it is not the same as consequence: none.

This is where the book ends and the post begins. Everything below is the self-check, an honest account of what this book does not get you, and what to do this week.

Did it work?

  • Did you record two different deadlines and not merge them? Three weeks for the debit and 28 May for an objection are different acts with different purposes. A single date on your page means the reading is not finished.
  • Did you notice that the payment section has two branches? Mits switches one on and indien switches the other on. If you wrote down one action, you read one branch and missed the condition attached to it.
  • Is every status one of chapter 08's five? This letter contains a requirement, a conditional requirement and an option. Calling all three required loses the difference between money leaving your account and a form you may choose not to send.
  • Did you grade the actors rather than guessing them? Is vastgesteld and is toegepast name nobody. The actor is recoverable, which is a different entry from stated.
  • Did the arithmetic close? € 3.245,- minus € 2.000,- is € 1.245,-, and it matches the headline. If it did not, chapter 12 says suspect your decode first.
  • Did you look up fewer than five words? Not a rule — a calibration. If you needed a dictionary for twenty, the structure did more work than you noticed, and it is worth going back to see which sentences you got through without one.

What this book does not get you

This is the last section of the last chapter and it is the honest one. Three things this letter contains are outside everything the book has taught, and no amount of rereading will bring them inside.

What the letter means legally. You can now find the sentence that requires payment, its date and its absent consequence. What an assessment obliges you to do, what happens if a period passes, whether an objection changes anything — none of that is printed on the page, and this book has said only what is printed. That was a deliberate limit, stated in chapter 13 and honoured here.

Whether the figures are right. Chapter 12 gave you a cross-check, and it is a real one: the letter's own arithmetic closes. That tells you that you decoded the numbers correctly. It tells you nothing whatever about whether € 3.245,- is the right amount of tax, which is a question about your circumstances and not about the page.

Vocabulary you were never given. Machtiging, dagtekening, heffingskorting — the book taught you to decompose these, which gets you a long way and does not get you a meaning. A compound you have split into two known parts is still a term of art whose sense you may have to look up. Splitting turns an unreadable word into a word you can look up, and that was always the claim.

The exit criterion for this book was always know what it wants, never know whether it is right. If you can read the letter above and say what is required of you and by when — with the uncertain parts marked as uncertain — the book has done what it set out to do, and the remaining questions are for someone with a different expertise.

Step 4 In your own post

There is no exercise this week that is not the real thing. Take the letter you have been avoiding. Not the easiest one in the pile — the one that has been sitting there because opening it costs something.

Run chapter 13's skim first. Then, only on the sentences that resist, go down the table in step 2 — the largest unit first, and no further than the obstruction. Expect to need three or four of the thirteen chapters, not all of them.

Run the same tests from step 3, and the last one honestly: count the words you looked up. Then write the five lines — status, actor, action, deadline, consequence — and mark every entry with how you know it.

Then record what stayed open. There will be some, and after fourteen chapters that is a result rather than a failure: a letter you have read carefully enough to say exactly which sentence you cannot settle is a letter you can ask one precise question about, instead of handing over the whole envelope.

Where this leaves you

The claim this book made at the start was narrow: not that vocabulary does not matter, but that a few recurring devices make words you already know hard to recognise, and that no course assembles them into a method for reading real documents. Fourteen chapters later the method is the table in step 2, and it fits on one page.

What it does not do is make you fluent, and it was never going to. It makes the post readable, which is a smaller thing and the thing that was actually in the way. The blue envelope is a letter now, not an event.

References

The letter is a reconstruction, composed for this book and modelled on a real definitieve aanslag. Every name, address, account number and amount is invented; the identifiers use placeholder forms. No sentence of the original appears in it.

It was audited before it was written into this chapter, sentence by sentence, against the chapter that explains each device — because the contents page promises that a device with no chapter is a defect in the spine rather than in the letter, and that promise is worth nothing if the audit is skipped. It found one: af, the marker in the margin of the calculation, was in the specimen and in no chapter. Chapter 12 now carries it.

Two constructions here are better than the real letter for teaching, and both were recorded as gaps long before this chapter was drafted. The original has het bedrag dat and no matching de-word with die, so chapter 05's contrast was only half attested; this letter adds de heffingskorting die is toegepast. And every dient te in the second specimen embeds a passive, which makes it a poor example of chapter 08 alone; dient u dit … door te geven is clean.

The source pointers behind the individual devices are in the chapters that introduced them. This chapter adds no new ones, which is what it means for a chapter to introduce nothing.

Terug

  • An article is followed by something no adjective could begin. Where is the noun it belongs to? (ch. 06)

    The first noun after the participle that closes the block — de door u ingediende aangifte. Not the first noun you meet, and not necessarily the word directly after the participle, since an ordinary adjective can still intervene.

  • A sentence ends in a short word that means nothing useful. What do you try? (ch. 03)

    Join it to the front of the clause and look the result up — schrijven … af is afschrijven. Confirm it rather than assume it: a stranded short word can also close a direction or period phrase.

  • Two conditions switch two different actions on in the same paragraph. Which chapter tells them apart, and how? (ch. 10)

    Chapter 10, by the verb in the clause beside them. Mits and indien are both subordinating, so both clauses are qualifications — and each one governs a different main clause, which is what makes the paragraph two branches rather than one instruction.

  • A letter requires money and states no consequence. What do you write on the consequence line? (ch. 08, 10)

    Not stated — which is not none. Chapter 10's test tells you whether the letter has settled the question, and here it has not; the requirement stands on what the document is, not on a sentence you can point at.

Nog niet

  • Listening and speaking. Out of scope from the first page, and a different skill with different methods
  • Writing Dutch. This book reads; it does not compose. Donaldson's letter appendix is the mirror image if you want it
  • The finite imperfect in narrative. Chapter 04 taught the participles, because that is what procedural documents contain. A decision letter that recounts a history uses the other half
  • What any of it means legally. Never in scope, and the one limit worth restating on the last page
