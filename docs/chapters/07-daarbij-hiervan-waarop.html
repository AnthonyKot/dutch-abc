Part II · What refers to what — Chapter 07

daarbij, hiervan, waarop

You were taught that daar means there and hier means here. In an official letter they usually mean neither. They are pronouns standing in for a thing, welded to a preposition and written backwards.

Before you start (ch. 06)

In a left-hand modifier block, how do you find the head noun — and why is it not simply the word after the participle?

Answer

Find the participle that closes the block, then continue to the first noun after it: an ordinary adjective can still intervene, as in het door u ingevulde nieuwe formulier. A nested article inside the block tells you to close that inner phrase and keep looking. Chapter 06 also promised you this chapter twice: for the full waar- family, and for the fact that these forms come apart.

The device Dutch cannot put a pronoun for a thing after a preposition. Where English says with it, about that, on which, Dutch replaces the pronoun with one of four pointer words, puts it in front of the preposition, and writes the two as one: ermee, daarover, waarop. Then, very often, it pulls them apart again and leaves the preposition at the far end of the clause.
Scope. This chapter is about pointer words used with a preposition. The word er has three other jobs in Dutch that have nothing to do with prepositions, and your course has already taught you all three. They are named at the end of step 2 so you can tell them apart, and then left alone.

Step 1 On the page

Two sentences from the middle of a letter. Every word in them is a word you know.

Betreft: uw bezwaar

Wij hebben u er in onze vorige brief al op gewezen dat de termijn op 1 juni afloopt.

De bijlage waarin de berekening staat, hoort hierbij.

Take the first one word by word, the way you would if it arrived on a Tuesday. Wij hebben u — we have you. er — there. in onze vorige brief — in our previous letter. Fine so far. al — already. op — on. On what? Nothing follows it except gewezen, and a preposition with no noun after it is not something the language is supposed to do.

Chapter 03 gave you one explanation for a short word stranded like this — it is half of a separable verb — and told you to carry it back and join it to the front of the infinitive. Do that here and you get opwijzen, which is not a Dutch word. The procedure has run and produced nothing, which is the honest signal that this is a different construction.

The second sentence stops the eye somewhere else. Waarin and hierbij are single words with no entry under waarin or hierbij in most learner dictionaries. Split at the obvious seam and you get where + in, and here + with, which sounds like a sentence about a location. Nothing in the letter is about a location. The annexe is not here in any physical sense; it is attached to this letter.

Both stops have the same cause. A pointer word and a preposition belong together, and the letter has either welded them into a word your dictionary does not list, or thrown them to opposite ends of the clause.

Step 2 What it is doing

The rule that forces all of this

In Dutch, a preposition cannot be followed by a pronoun standing for a thing. op het, van het, met het are not stylistically poor — they are impossible. English has no equivalent restriction, which is why the construction has no shape you can borrow.

So Dutch does two things at once. It replaces the pronoun with a pointer word, and it puts that pointer before the preposition instead of after it. The result is written as a single word.

met het → ✗  ·  daar + met → daarmee "with it" becomes, literally, "that-with". The order reverses and the two words fuse.

Note what happened to met on the way in. It becomes mee inside these forms — never daarmet. That is the one shape change frequent enough to be worth memorising; a small number of other prepositions do something similar, and the reliable habit is simply to try the plain form too when a lookup fails.

The four pointers

Which pointer appears depends on what it points at, and the distinctions are the ones you already know from dit, dat and die.

PointerStands forExampleReads as
er it — unstressed, pointing at something already mentioned ervan of it, from it
hier dit — this, the thing at hand hierbij with this, herewith
daar dat — that, the thing just named daarover about that
waar which — inside a relative clause, or a question waarop on which

The last row is not new. Chapter 06 used waarvan and waarin as relative pronouns and said the family would be explained here. This is the explanation: waar- is what Dutch uses instead of die or dat when the relative pronoun would have to sit after a preposition, and it is used for things whether the noun is a de-word or a het-word. For a person, Dutch keeps the preposition in front and uses wie — aan wie, met wie — which is also chapter 06's rule, now with its reason attached.

And then they come apart

This is the half that makes them hard to read rather than merely hard to look up. The pointer and the preposition are separable, and in ordinary written Dutch they usually are separated. The pointer moves up to just after the finite verb; the preposition stays behind at the end of the clause.

daarmee daarmee Written joined, it is one word. Written apart — daar heb ik het mee geschreven — the two halves can sit a whole clause from each other, and the second half looks exactly like a stranded preposition.

There is one detail about where the preposition lands, and it is the detail that makes chapter 02 pay off here. The preposition goes to the end of the clause, but it stops before a past participle or an infinitive. It does not go past the verb; it goes to the last position inside the bracket. So in a perfect-tense clause you will find it immediately in front of the participle, not after it.

Looking at the very last word

Wij hebben u er … op gewezen

The last word is gewezen, a participle. Reading it as the end of the search leaves op unexplained, floating one word earlier with nothing after it.

Looking at the end of the bracket

Wij hebben u [er … op] gewezen

The bracket closes with gewezen; the preposition is the last thing inside it. er + op = erop, and the verb is wijzen op, to point out.

Why so many of these verbs demand a preposition

A large group of Dutch verbs is permanently attached to a particular preposition, in the way English depends on and consists of are. wijzen op, reageren op, denken aan, overtuigen van, deelnemen aan. The preposition is not doing its ordinary work there — it is simply part of the verb's frame, and the verb is incomplete without it.

When the object of such a verb is a thing already mentioned, every one of these produces a pointer form: erop gewezen, eraan gedacht, ervan overtuigd. That is why official letters are saturated with them. A letter's whole job is referring back to things it has already named.

The three uses this chapter is not about

Your course taught you er, and taught it correctly, but taught the other jobs it does. Er with a number — ik heb er drie, I have three of them. Er as a place — ik woon er, I live there, where hier and daar genuinely do mean here and there. And er with an indefinite subject — er ligt een brief op tafel, there is a letter on the table.

None of those has a preposition welded to it, and that is the whole test. This chapter is about the fourth job. It is also the one your course did not cover, which is worth knowing: if these forms have never looked familiar, that is not a gap in your attention.

Step 3 Read it

The procedure

  1. Spot a pointer. A word that is or begins with er, hier, daar or waar.
  2. Find its preposition. Either it is welded on the right, or it is stranded later in the clause — at the end of the bracket, just before any participle or infinitive.
  3. Reverse them and read them as English. waarin → in which. erop → on it. hierbij → with this.
  4. Find what it points at — and look in both directions. hier and daar point at this thing and that thing. waar- points at the noun in front of the relative clause. er usually points back at something already named — but it can also point forward, at a dat-clause or an om … te-clause later in the sentence. See the box below before you go looking behind it.

The pointer that points forwards

Step 1's first sentence is the trap, and it is worth being caught by it deliberately.

Wij hebben u er in onze vorige brief al op gewezen dat de termijn op 1 juni afloopt.

Everything in the procedure runs correctly: the pointer is er, the preposition is op at the end of the bracket, and joined they give on it. Then step 4 sends you looking backwards for the thing it stands for — and there is nothing there. The deadline has not been mentioned yet. It arrives after the verb, in the dat-clause.

That is not a failure of the analysis; it is a second use of the same construction. When a verb demands a preposition and its object is a whole clause rather than a noun, Dutch cannot simply write op dat …. It puts a placeholder er in the pointer position, strands the preposition as usual, and lets the dat-clause deliver the content afterwards.

Wij hebben u erop gewezen dat … "We pointed out to you on it — namely, that …". The erop is holding a place open, and the clause fills it.

So the sentence says: we already pointed out to you in our previous letter that the deadline expires on 1 June. In a letter about an objection that is not a neutral sentence — it is the letter establishing that you were warned.

The signal is mechanical. If a pointer has no referent behind it, look ahead for a dat or an om … te in the same sentence before concluding anything else. This shape is extremely common in official Dutch, because a letter's business is asserting things about whole situations rather than about objects: erop wijzen dat, ervan uitgaan dat, erop aandringen dat.

The second sentence in step 1 is the ordinary case by comparison, and both of its pointers behave the way step 4 leads you to expect. Waarin is a pointer plus in, giving in which, pointing at de bijlage in front of it — the annexe in which the calculation appears. hierbij is with this, meaning enclosed with this letter. Neither word was about a place.

Did it work?

You have found a pointer, matched it to a preposition, and named what it refers to. Five tests, and the first three can each tell you outright that the reading is wrong.

  • The second half must be a real preposition. Split your candidate at the seam and check: waar+in, daar+over, hier+bij. If what is left is not a preposition, this is an ordinary word that happens to start with those letters, and Dutch has plenty — daarom, waarom and waarschijnlijk are not members of this family however much they look like it.
  • It is not a place. If your reading of hier or daar is here or there as a location, and a preposition is attached to it, you have applied the rule your course taught for a different job. Substitute this and that instead. A letter is almost never telling you where something is.
  • Chapter 03's test, run backwards — and there are three possibilities, not two. If a short word is stranded at the end of a clause, join it to the infinitive first. A real dictionary verb means it was a separable verb and this chapter does not apply. A non-word — opwijzen — means look elsewhere, and elsewhere has two addresses: a pointer earlier in the clause, or a direction or period phrase immediately to the left of the short word, as in de grens over. Check both before deciding. The absence of a pointer does not prove you are holding a particle, and chapter 03 says so explicitly.
  • Look before the participle, not after it. The stranded preposition sits at the end of the bracket. In a perfect-tense clause that is one word before the participle. If you searched only the final word of the sentence and found nothing, search again one position earlier.
  • The referent must exist — behind or ahead. If nothing before the pointer can be what it stands for, look forward for a dat or an om … te in the same sentence before concluding anything: the pointer may be holding a place open for a clause that has not arrived yet. Only when there is no referent in either direction, and no preposition attached, are you looking at one of the three uses this chapter fenced off — and concluding that is a correct outcome, not a failure.

Three to try

Find every pointer, match it to its preposition, and say what it refers to. Write the answers down before opening the worked versions. One of the three contains a word that looks like a member of this family and is not.

  1. Wij hebben uw brief ontvangen en zullen er binnen drie weken op reageren.
  2. Het formulier waarmee u bezwaar maakt, vindt u op onze website. Daarom sturen wij het niet mee.
  3. Wij hebben u hierover in maart geschreven, maar wij hebben daarop geen antwoord ontvangen.
Show the worked answers

One. The pointer is er, and its preposition is op — nine words later, sitting immediately before the infinitive reageren, exactly where the bracket ends. Joined, erop, to it. The verb is reageren op, to respond to, and the thing responded to is uw brief in the first clause. Note that chapter 03's test fails cleanly here: opreageren is not a word.

Two. Two candidates, and only one is a member of this family. Waarmee is waar + mee — with which — pointing at het formulier in front of it, and note mee rather than met, the shape change from step 2. It is a relative clause, so its verb goes to the end (maakt), which is chapter 02 and chapter 06 working exactly as before.

But daarom is not one of these. Split it and you get daar + om, and although om is a preposition, daarom is a fixed adverb meaning therefore, that is why. It points at a reason, not at a noun, and no preposition is doing prepositional work. This is the one the first test in the box is for.

Three. Both are genuine, and they show the hier / daar contrast doing real work. Hierover — about this, the matter at hand, the subject of the letter you are holding. Daarop — to that, pointing back at the March letter just mentioned. Two pointers, two different referents, and the difference between them is the difference between dit and dat. Both are written joined here, which is also normal; splitting is common, not compulsory.

Where this procedure cannot decide

Three of these forms are also ordinary adverbs of time, with a completely different meaning, and written joined they are identical.

daarvoor As a time adverb: before that. As a pointer plus preposition: for that. Same six letters.

Wij hebben daarvoor geen kosten in rekening gebracht.

Read it as a pointer and the sentence says we have not charged any costs for that — the item is free. Read it as a time adverb and it says we had not charged any costs before that — the item may well be charged now. In a letter about money those are not variants of one meaning; they are opposite instructions about whether you owe something.

One thing does decide it, and only when it is present: the split. The time adverbs — daarna, daarop in its thereupon sense, and daarvoor — are never pulled apart. So daar heb ik geen kosten voor berekend can only be the pointer. Written joined, both readings survive and the form itself tells you nothing.

This is the same shape as chapter 03's voorkomen: when the word splits, the split is the evidence; when it does not split, there may be no evidence at all. What usually breaks the tie is the verb rather than the pointer — if the verb demands a particular preposition, that preposition is spoken for. But when the verb demands nothing, mark the sentence as carrying two readings and go on. A reader who records the ambiguity will check it; a reader who resolves it by instinct has a fifty per cent chance and no way of knowing which half they are in.

Step 4 In your own post

This chapter has the most mechanical search in the book. Go through a letter and circle every word beginning er, hier, daar or waar. There will be more of them than you expect — in a formal letter these words are the connective tissue, because the letter's business is referring back to things it has already named.

For each one, ask only whether a preposition is attached or stranded. That single question sorts the whole list into this chapter's forms and the three jobs it fenced off, and it takes a second per word.

Two places repay it especially. Any sentence enclosing or referring to a document — hierbij, bijgaand, waarin — because that is the standard vocabulary for saying what is attached to what. And any sentence about a previous letter, which is where daarop, hierover and daarvan cluster. A letter that refers back to earlier correspondence is usually the letter that is escalating, and these words are how it does the referring.

Run the same five tests from step 3 on every form you find, and run the third one — chapter 03's separable-verb test, backwards — on every stranded short word before you decide it belongs to a pointer. The two constructions look identical on the page, and the dictionary is what separates them.

Then record what stayed open, in two piles. Any er you could not find a referent for, which probably belongs to one of the fenced-off uses. And every unsplit daarvoor, daarop or daarna where the verb did not demand a preposition — those carry two readings, and in a sentence about money or deadlines the second pile is the one worth taking to somebody who knows.

Part II ends here — one passage using all of it

Part II asked one question in three chapters: what refers to what? Chapter 05 gave you the gender tag on a pointer, chapter 06 gave you modifier blocks on both sides of a noun, and this chapter gave you the pointer-plus-preposition family. The passage below needs all three at once, and chapter 02 underneath them. It is not a new device; it is the four working together, which is the only form in which you will ever meet them.

Betreft: uw aanvraag

De door u ingediende aanvraag, waarin u om uitstel vraagt, hebben wij op 12 mei ontvangen. Wij hebben er nog niet op beslist.

De bijlage die u heeft meegestuurd, bevat een berekening waarvan de datum ontbreekt. Wij vragen u daarom een nieuwe bijlage op te sturen. Zonder die gegevens kunnen wij uw aanvraag niet beoordelen.

Show the worked reading

Sentence one. The phrase opens with De followed by door, which is chapter 06's signal: an article followed by material no ordinary adjective could begin with. The participle closing the block is ingediende, so the head is the first noun after it, aanvraag — the application submitted by you. Then a relative clause on the right, waarin u om uitstel vraagt, using this chapter's waarin to point back at that same aanvraag, with its verb at the end by chapter 02. The main clause's own verbs are hebben early and ontvangen at the end, eleven words apart.

Sentence two. er … op, split, with op sitting immediately before the participle beslist — the end of the bracket, not the end of the line. The verb is beslissen op, and what has not been decided on is the application. This is the sentence that matters and it is five words long.

Sentence three. die points at de bijlage — a de-word, so die and not dat, which is chapter 05. Then waarvan points at een berekening, the nearer noun, giving of which: the calculation whose date is missing.

Sentence four. daarom — and by the first test in the check box, this is not a member of the family. It is the fixed adverb therefore. The sentence also carries a separable verb from chapter 03, opsturen, split around te as op te sturen.

Sentence five. die gegevens — die again, but this time as a demonstrative in front of a plural noun rather than a relative pointer. Chapter 05 warned that in the plural the form is forced and tells you nothing; here what tells you it is a demonstrative is that no clause follows it and there is no gap.

The letter's actual content, once assembled: your application arrived on 12 May, no decision has been taken, a date is missing from the annexe, send a new one, and nothing will be assessed until you do. Four of the five sentences are administrative scaffolding. The one that requires you to act is the fourth, and it is neither first nor last — which is chapter 13's whole subject.

References

Donaldson gives Er a chapter of its own, printed pages 235–238, and opens it by naming four separate functions — repletive, partitive, pronominal and locative. This chapter is about the third of those, §15.3 at printed 236, which states the restriction the whole construction follows from: op het, van het and the like are impossible in Dutch, and that op ze or van ze can refer only to people, never to things. The placement rule used in step 2 is his: the pointer goes immediately after the finite verb and the preposition to the end of the clause, but before past participles and infinitives.

The forward-pointing use in step 3 is on the same page. Donaldson introduces it as a "complicated usage" of pronominal er arising with verbs that take a fixed preposition, and every example he gives for it has the same shape: the pointer, then the stranded preposition, then a dat-clause or a te-infinitive carrying the actual content. He does not label the direction, but the examples are unanimous, and the pattern is one of the commonest sentence shapes in Dutch officialese.

The hier- and daar- forms are §8.4.3 at printed 62, which gives the substitution directly — daar + met = daarmee — states that these forms are separable, and lists the exception this chapter's unresolved case is built on: the time adverbs daarna, daarop and daarvoor are not separated. The waar- relatives are §8.5.3 at printed 63, the same section chapter 06 used for wie. The verbs permanently attached to a preposition are §11.23, printed 185–189, grouped under the Dutch preposition rather than the verb — which is the arrangement a reader needs and a dictionary does not provide.

De Opmaat covers er in three subsections, printed 263–264: er with a numeral, er as a place, and er with an indefinite subject. Those are three of Donaldson's four functions. The prepositional one — the subject of this chapter — is not among them, and Taaltalent 2 does not reach it either. This is the clearest instance in the book of a construction that saturates written Dutch and is absent from the courses that precede it, and it is worth being explicit that the omission is reasonable: both books are building toward speaking, and a learner can talk for a long time without needing waarvan. A reader meets it in the first paragraph of the first formal letter.

One thing the older source shows its age on: De Opmaat teaches hier and daar as substitutes for er meaning place, and it is right to. But that is precisely the training that makes hierbij and daarvan unreadable, because it installs here and there as the first meaning that comes to mind. The course is not wrong; it is incomplete in a way that actively misleads a reader, which is a different thing and worth naming.

Kaart

Nieuw

  • A preposition cannot take a pronoun for a thing: op het, van het are impossible
  • Instead: pointer before preposition, written as one word
  • The four pointers — er (it), hier (dit), daar (dat), waar (which)
  • met becomes mee inside these forms
  • They split, and usually do: pointer after the finite verb, preposition at the end of the clause
  • The preposition stops before a participle or infinitive — the end of the bracket, not the end of the line
  • Verbs permanently attached to a preposition are what generate these: wijzen op, reageren op, denken aan
  • er can point forwards, holding a place open for a dat- or om … te-clause that arrives later — erop wijzen dat, ervan uitgaan dat. No referent behind it is a signal, not a dead end
  • waar- for things, wie for people — chapter 06's rule, with its reason
  • Not members of the family: daarom, waarom, waarschijnlijk
  • The three fenced-off uses of er: with a numeral, as a place, with an indefinite subject

Terug

  • A short word is stranded at the end of a clause. What do you do first, and how many things can it turn out to be? (ch. 03)

    Join it to the infinitive and look the result up. Three outcomes: a dictionary verb means a separable verb; otherwise it belongs either to a pointer earlier in the clause or to a direction or period phrase on its immediate left — de grens over. The failed lookup is evidence, not a dead end.

  • Where does the verb bracket actually close, and why does that matter for finding a stranded preposition? (ch. 02)

    It closes on the participle or infinitive. The stranded preposition is the last thing inside the bracket, so it sits one word before that — not at the end of the line.

  • Why can a relative clause not simply use die or dat after a preposition? (ch. 06)

    Because a preposition cannot take a thing-pronoun at all. Dutch uses waar- plus the preposition instead — and wie when the antecedent is a person.

  • When a form can be read two ways and it is written unsplit, what is the correct outcome? (ch. 03)

    Record both readings. The split is the evidence; with no split there may be no evidence, and choosing by instinct is a coin toss you cannot audit.
