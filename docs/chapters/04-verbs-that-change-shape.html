Part I · What makes a known word unrecognisable — Chapter 04

Verbs that change shape

The verb you found at the end of the clause is not in the dictionary. Two independent things have happened to it: something was done to the front of the word, and something may have been done to its middle. Ask about them separately and most of these forms come apart in seconds.

Before you start (ch. 03)

You reach the end of a clause and find uit. What do you do with it, and what tells you the result is right?

Answer

Carry it back and join it to the infinitive of the finite verb — stellen … uit gives uitstellen, not uitstel and not uitstellt. What tells you it is right is that the joined form is a dictionary entry. This chapter is about what happens to that verb once it stops being an infinitive.

The device Dutch reshapes a verb to make a past participle, along two axes at once. The word's structure decides where ge- goes — in front, inside after a particle, or nowhere — and that tells you where the dictionary verb begins. The verb's inflection decides whether the stem survived intact, and that tells you whether you can recover it yourself or must look it up. The two answers are independent, and you need both.

Step 1 On the page

Two sentences from the explaining part of a letter — the part that says what has already been done to your case.

In the style of an assessment letter

Wij hebben uw aangifte gecontroleerd en de aanslag opnieuw vastgesteld.

Wij hebben uw brief op 3 juni ontvangen en uw bezwaar op 10 juni beoordeeld.

Chapter 02 sent you to the end of the clause and chapter 03 told you what to do if a particle was waiting there. Here the ends of the clauses are occupied by four words that are plainly verbs and plainly not infinitives. Search a dictionary for gecontroleerd, vastgesteld or beoordeeld and you will find nothing under those spellings.

They are not obscure words. Every one of them is an ordinary verb wearing the standard Dutch past-participle costume, and the costume differs in each case: gecontroleerd has gained a syllable at the front, vastgesteld has gained one in the middle, beoordeeld has gained nothing, and ontvangen — which is in the dictionary — is not there as a participle at all. It is the entry for the infinitive, which happens to be spelled the same way.

This is Part I's question for the fourth and last time. Chapter 01: the word was welded to another word. Chapter 03: the word was cut in half. Here: the word has been reshaped. Same failure, same reader — and this is the only one of the three where part of the answer is a list you have to acquire rather than a rule you can apply.

Step 2 What it is doing

The regular machine

Most Dutch verbs are weak, which means their past forms are manufactured rather than remembered. The stem keeps its vowel, and an ending is bolted on:

controleren → gecontroleerd Past participle: ge- in front, -d or -t at the back, stem unchanged in between.

Whether it ends -d or -t depends on whether the stem ends in a voiceless consonant. Dutch schools teach this as 't kofschip, whose consonants — t, k, f, s, ch, p — are exactly the voiceless ones that take -t. For a reader this matters hardly at all: both endings mean the same thing, and you are going to strip whichever one is there.

That is the useful direction. You are never building these forms; you are undoing them. Everything below is written backwards from the page to the dictionary.

Two questions, not one list

It is tempting to sort these forms into a single list of types. Do not — the two things that happen to a verb are independent, and a form can be anywhere in the grid:

Weak: stem intactStrong: stem changed
Simple verb gecontroleerd → controleren geschreven → schrijven
Separable vastgesteld → vaststellen afgewezen → afwijzen
Inseparable beoordeeld → beoordelen overschreden → overschrijden

Read down a column and the structure changes while the stem behaves the same way. Read across a row and the structure is fixed while the stem does or does not survive. The left-hand question tells you where the dictionary verb begins. The top question tells you whether you can recover it yourself. Answering one does not answer the other, and this is the whole reason the procedure in step 3 has more than one pass.

The second and third rows are chapter 03 arriving in a new costume, and they use the same two lists. The particles that split a verb are the particles that ge- gets inserted behind — invullen gives ingevuld, overmaken gives overgemaakt, uitstellen gives uitgesteld. And the six inseparable prefixes are the six that block ge- entirely: be-, er-, ge-, her-, ont-, ver-. If a verb already begins with one of those, its participle looks like the stem with an ending and nothing else — verrekend, behandeld, hersteld.

So the two lists you learned in chapter 03 for finding the verb now also tell you how to get back to it. That is the whole reason chapter 03 comes first.

The six are not the whole story, and chapter 03 already told you why. The variable prefixes — voor-, over-, door-, om-, onder- — block ge- too when they are being used inseparably. That is how you get overschreden alongside overgemaakt: same five letters at the front, one of them taking a ge- in the middle and the other refusing one altogether. In chapter 03 you told these apart by whether the verb split. Here you tell them apart by where the ge- is — and the two tests always agree, because they are testing the same fact.

So the rule is not "one of six". It is: if there is no ge- anywhere, the verb begins with an unstressed prefix, which is either one of the six or one of the variable ones doing its inseparable job.

The trap in stripping ge-

One consequence of that list catches everybody. Ge- is itself one of the six inseparable prefixes, so a verb whose infinitive already begins with ge- does not get a second one:

gebruiken → gebruikt Strip the ge- and you are left with bruiken, which is not a word. The ge- belongs to the verb.
gebeuren → gebeurd Worse, because stripping gives beuren, which is a word — a rare one, about collecting money. A wrong answer that survives the dictionary.

The check is cheap but it has to be done on the sentence, not on the word: strip the ge-, and if what remains is not a word, or is a word that makes no sense where you found it, put the ge- back and look up the form as it stands.

Restoring the spelling

Stripping the ends often leaves you with a stem rather than an infinitive, and Dutch respells stems. Three alternations do nearly all of the damage, and all three run backwards as easily as forwards:

  • Doubled vowel becomes single. gemaakt → maak → maken.
  • Single consonant becomes doubled. gestopt → stop → stoppen.
  • Final f and s become v and z. geleefd → leef → leven; gereisd → reis → reizen.

If a stripped stem is not in the dictionary, one of those three is almost always the reason, and trying them takes seconds.

Strong verbs, and the thing that is not derivable

A minority of verbs — but a very frequent minority — are strong: they form their past by changing the vowel in the stem rather than by adding an ending. Ontvangen gives ontving; blijken gives bleek; schrijven gives schreef. No rule generates these. They are a list, and it is the one piece of memorisation this book asks for.

There is one reliable signal for spotting them, and it is at the other end of the word. A weak participle ends in -d or -t; a strong one ends in -n — usually -en. Compare beoordeeld and verrekend against geschreven, gebleken and ontvangen. The ending tells you which half of the grid you are in before you have worked out anything else, and therefore whether you are about to reverse a rule or reach for a table.

⚠ One class defeats that signal, and it is worth knowing it exists rather than meeting it cold. A few very common verbs take a weak -t ending and change their stem: brengen gives gebracht, denken gives gedacht, kopen gives gekocht. These look reversible and are not — strip and restore gebracht and you get nothing usable. They belong in the table with the strong verbs, and the sign that you need it is precisely that the reversal produced a non-word.

Two corrections to the intuition, because the tidy version of this is false.

First, the past tense does not predict the participle. They are two separate facts about a verb and have to be learned as a triple:

worden — werd — geworden The vowel goes o, then e, then back to o. Knowing werd would not have told you geworden.
schrijven — schreef — geschreven Three different vowels in three forms of one verb.

Second, the two halves can come from different systems. A verb can be strong in one form and weak in the other, so "is it strong?" is not even a well-formed question about some verbs:

vragen — vroeg — gevraagd Strong past tense, weak participle.
bakken — bakte — gebakken Weak past tense, strong participle. The other way round.

The practical consequence is small but real: when a strong form defeats you, look it up in a table of three columns rather than trying to reason from one form to another. Donaldson's list is at printed pages 125–135.

What you will actually meet, and what you will not

Everything above splits into two halves that are not equally common in your post, and it is worth saying which is which rather than implying both arrive daily.

Participles are everywhere. The two documents this book works from — a tax assessment and a government form — are full of them: ingevuld, vastgesteld, gebaseerd, toegekend, uitbetaald, ontvangen, verrekend.

The finite past tense is not. Neither document contains a single one, and that is not an accident of sampling. Both are procedural: they set out standing rules and required actions, which is present-tense work. The past tense turns up where a letter recounts something — a decision that explains its own history, a rejection that recites what you sent and when. If your post is mostly forms and assessments, you will meet ontvangen a hundred times before you meet ontving once. Learn the participles first.

Step 3 Read it

Three passes

Given a verb form you cannot find, run all three of these. They are passes, not alternatives: each answers a different question, and skipping one is how you end up with a plausible wrong answer.

  1. What job is the form doing? Chapter 02 already tells you. A form at the end of a clause, governed by a finite hebben, zijn or worden near the front, is a participle. A form in second position is a finite verb. A bare form at the end under a modal is an infinitive. (Worden here does something particular to the sentence, which is chapter 09.)
  2. Where does the dictionary verb begin? Find the ge-. At the front — an ordinary participle, so remove it. Inside, straight after a short particle — cut there, and the particle goes to the front of whatever infinitive you recover from the rest. Absent entirely — the verb starts with an unstressed prefix, either one of the six or a variable prefix doing its inseparable job, and there is nothing to remove from the front at all.
  3. Can the ending be reversed? If the form ends in -d or -t, strip it, undo the spelling, add -en, and check the result is a word. If it ends in -n, or if the reversal produced a non-word, the stem has changed and you need the strong-and-irregular table. Then test the verb you recovered against the sentence.

Pass two locates the verb. Pass three recovers it. Neither substitutes for the other, which is why the earlier grid has two axes: knowing that afgewezen is separable does not tell you that gewezen comes from wijzen.

Run all three on step 1. Gecontroleerd: participle, ge- at the front, ends in -d — reverse it to controleren, done in one pass each. Vastgesteld: participle; ge- in the middle, so cut to vast plus gesteld; that ends in -d and reverses to stellen, giving vaststellen — to determine, in the sense in which a tax office determines a figure. Beoordeeld: participle; no ge- anywhere, so the verb starts with an unstressed prefix and nothing comes off the front; ends in -d, reversing to beoordelen, to assess.

Ontvangen is the one that needs all three passes and would defeat any of them alone. Pass two: no ge-, so ont- is an unstressed prefix and the whole word is the verb. Pass three: it ends in -n, so the stem has changed and this is a strong verb — which is why its participle is spelled exactly like its infinitive. That leaves nothing on the page at all to distinguish to receive from received, and only pass one settles it: Wij hebben … ontvangen has a finite hebben in second position, so the form at the end of the clause is a participle. Chapter 02 does work here that no amount of staring at the word could do.

The wrong parse

The characteristic error here is the one from chapter 03 in a new place: a lookup that succeeds, and is wrong.

U heeft uw inkomen uit het buitenland niet aangegeven.

Strip the ge- you can see

gegeven → geven

To give. "You did not give your foreign income." Odd, but it reads like an administrative slip — a missing figure, a form to resend.

Cut at the ge- instead

aan + gegeven → aangeven

To declare. "You did not declare your foreign income." That is not a missing figure. That is what a letter says before it says what happens next.

Both readings involve a correct dictionary lookup of a real Dutch word. The difference is entirely pass two: the ge- was in the middle, so it was a seam, not a prefix.

Three to try

Run all three passes on every verb form, in order, and write down the dictionary infinitive for each before you open the answers. One of these three sentences contains no participle at all, and noticing that is pass one doing its job.

  1. De inspecteur heeft uw verzoek afgewezen omdat u de termijn heeft overschreden.
  2. Wij hebben de heffingskorting op uw inkomen gebaseerd en het bedrag opnieuw berekend.
  3. Vorig jaar ontving u een voorlopige aanslag, maar dit jaar bleek uw inkomen hoger.
Show the worked answers

One. This is the pair that shows why the passes cannot be collapsed: the two verbs differ on both axes, and each axis is settled by a different pass.

Afgewezen — pass two finds a ge- in the middle after a particle, so cut it: af plus gewezen. That is as far as pass two goes, and it has not given you the verb yet. Pass three: the remainder ends in -n, so the stem has changed and the table is required — it gives wijzen, and only now can the particle go back on to make afwijzen, to reject.

Overschreden — pass two finds no ge- at all, so the verb begins with an unstressed prefix and nothing comes off the front. Note that over- is not one of the six; it is one of the variable prefixes from chapter 03, here doing its inseparable job, and the missing ge- is exactly the evidence of that. Pass three: it ends in -n, so again the table, which gives overschrijden, to exceed.

In chapter 03 you told the two over-verbs apart by whether they split. Here you tell them apart by where the ge- is, or is not. The two tests always agree, because both are reading the same fact off the page.

Two. Two participles side by side, both weak, differing only in structure. Gebaseerd has its ge- at the front and ends in -d: both passes are routine, giving baseren. Berekend has no ge- — be- blocks it — and also ends in -d, so pass three reverses cleanly, giving berekenen, to calculate. One has a ge- and one does not, and the difference is not tense or emphasis. It is the first two letters of the verb.

Three. Pass one does all the work here, and it is the register you meet less often, so it is worth seeing once. Ontving and bleek are finite past tenses, not participles, and you know that before looking at either word: they sit in second position rather than at the end, with no auxiliary anywhere. Ontving is ontvangen with the vowel changed and bleek is blijken, to appear or turn out. Neither is derivable and both are on the list. A letter that opens like this is recounting a history, which is what tells you it is a decision rather than a form.

Did it work?

You have turned a form on the page into a dictionary infinitive. Five tests, and the fourth is the one most likely to change your mind about an answer you were happy with.

  • Pass one really did run. A participle in the verb bracket needs an auxiliary in its clause — a form of hebben, zijn or worden. If you bracketed the clause by chapter 02 and found no auxiliary, what you are holding is a finite past tense, and passes two and three will happily give you a confident wrong answer about a word that was never a participle. (One exception, and it is the one at the end of step 4: a participle sitting in front of a noun, usually with an -e, is not in the verb bracket at all and has no auxiliary to find. It is still a participle, and the three passes still work on it.)
  • The result must be an infinitive. It ends in -en (a few in -n: zijn, doen, gaan). If your answer ends in -d or -t, you stopped one step early.
  • The passes must agree with chapter 03. If pass two found ge- in the middle, the material in front of it must be a particle you would recognise from chapter 03 — af, in, uit, op, terug, mee. If it is not, you cut in the wrong place.
  • The reversal test is one-directional, and this is the important one. A reversal that produces a non-word is proof you were wrong: strip the ge- from gebruikt and bruiken is nothing, so the ge- belonged to the verb. But a reversal that produces a real word proves nothing at all. See the next box before you trust one.
  • The verb must fit the sentence. Not just exist. If your recovered infinitive is a real verb whose meaning makes the clause odd, that is evidence against the analysis, not evidence that the letter is strangely written.

Where this procedure cannot decide

Pass two asks where ge- is. It cannot ask whether the ge- it found is the participle marker or the first two letters of the verb, because those two things look identical.

gebruikt → bruiken ✗ → gebruiken Strip the ge- and nothing is left that is a word. The failure is the answer: ge- was part of the verb.
gebeurd → beuren ✓ → gebeuren Strip the ge- and beuren is a real Dutch verb — to lift, or to collect money. Every test passes. The answer is still wrong: the verb is gebeuren, to happen.

The two lines above are the same operation with the same outcome — a word — and only one of them is right. Nothing in the form of gebeurd distinguishes it from a participle of beuren, which would be spelled the same way. The procedure has produced a real verb, a plausible reading, and no signal whatever that it has gone wrong.

So the honest rule is asymmetric. A reversal that fails has told you something; a reversal that succeeds has told you almost nothing. When a stripped ge- leaves a word behind, check the meaning against the sentence, and if both readings survive, check both against the dictionary — the six inseparable prefixes include ge- for exactly this reason, and verbs beginning with it are the standing trap of this chapter.

Step 4 In your own post

Part I ends here, so this week's exercise is the whole of it. Take a letter and go through it once with all four chapters at once: split the long nouns, find the finite verb, check the end of the clause for a stranded particle, and run the three passes on any verb form you cannot look up. Those moves between them account for most of what makes an ordinary Dutch sentence unreadable to someone who knows the words.

The participles cluster in one place, and it is worth knowing which. The explaining paragraphs — the ones headed with something like Hoe wij dit hebben berekend — are almost entirely participles, because their job is to report what has already been done to your case. Skimming a letter for participles is a fast way to find the paragraph that says what happened, as opposed to the paragraphs that say what the rules are.

One thing to expect and not be thrown by: participles also appear in front of nouns, with an -e on the end — het ingevulde formulier, het vastgestelde bedrag. That is the same word doing a different job, and chapter 06 is about the job. For now, recognising the participle underneath the -e is enough, and the three passes find it unchanged.

Run the same five tests from step 3 on every form you recover. Because this is a whole-of-Part-I pass, run the third one especially — the one that requires pass two's cut to agree with chapter 03's particle list. It is the only test here that checks one chapter's answer against another's, and a disagreement means one of the two readings is wrong.

Then write down what stayed open, and expect three kinds. A form whose reversal produced a non-word: that is a strong verb and the answer is in the table, not in you — resolved, just not by reasoning. A form whose reversal produced a real word that does not fit the sentence: unresolved, and most likely the ge- trap. And a form you could not classify in pass one at all, because you were not sure where its clause ended — that is chapter 02's limit surfacing again, and naming it is the correct answer rather than a gap in this chapter.

Part I closes with a fair statement of what it has given you. You can now reduce most unfamiliar words in an official letter to something you can look up, and you can tell, much of the time, when you have failed to. What it has not given you is the meaning of a sentence — only its parts, and the standing right to record that a particular sentence is not yet settled.

References

Donaldson's alphabetical list of strong and irregular verbs is at printed pages 125–135. It is the only part of this book's apparatus that is worth photographing and keeping on a phone: three columns, and the middle and right columns cannot be predicted from the left.

The teaching order in the courses is worth knowing, because it explains a gap. In Taaltalent, the perfect — the tense that uses these participles — is taught in deel 1 across two units, and the past tense proper is left to deel 2. De Opmaat's grammar reference covers both, plus a list of irregular verbs. So the forms are taught, and taught early. What is not taught anywhere is the reverse operation: how to get from a form on a page back to the entry you can look up, which is the only operation a reader ever needs.

Kaart

Nieuw

  • Structure and inflection are independent axes, so a form can be separable and strong
  • Where the ge- is tells you where the verb begins; the ending tells you whether you can reverse it
  • Weak participle ends -d/-t; strong ends -n
  • Separable verbs insert ge- after the particle
  • No ge- at all means an unstressed prefix — one of the six, or a variable prefix used inseparably
  • The past tense does not predict the participle; some verbs mix the two systems
  • gebracht, gedacht, gekocht look reversible and are not
  • Undoing the spelling: doubled vowel, doubled consonant, f/s → v/z
  • Participles are common in your post; the finite past tense is not

Terug

  • Which two lists from chapter 03 does this chapter use — and what has changed about how you use them? (ch. 03)

    The particles and the six inseparable prefixes. Chapter 03 used them to decide whether to join a stranded word; this chapter uses them backwards, to decide where to cut.

  • What tells you a clause-final form is a participle rather than a finite past tense? (ch. 02)

    An auxiliary earlier in the same clause governing it. No auxiliary, and a form standing in second position, means a finite past.

  • Chapter 01 undid a word to find its parts. What is the equivalent check here? (ch. 01)

    That the pieces go back together into a real dictionary entry — except that here a successful reassembly is much weaker evidence, because a wrong cut can still produce a real verb.

Nog niet

  • Participles with -e standing in front of a noun (ch. 06)
  • Whole clauses wedged between an article and its noun (ch. 06)
  • Participles in sentences with no actor at all (ch. 09)
  • Participles stacked inside a formal obligation (ch. 08)
