Part II · What refers to what — Chapter 05

de, het, die, dat

You were taught that de and het matter because saying the wrong one is embarrassing. For a reader they matter for a completely different reason, and it is a much better one.

Before you start (ch. 04)

You strip the ge- from a participle and what is left is a real Dutch verb. What have you proved?

Answer

Almost nothing. The test is one-directional: a reversal that leaves a non-word proves the ge- belonged to the verb, but a reversal that leaves a real word can still be wrong — gebeurd gives beuren, which exists, and is not the answer. This chapter's tests have the same shape: most of them can only tell you that you are wrong.

The device Every Dutch noun carries a gender tag, and the word that points back at a noun later in the sentence is normally spelled to match. Dat points at a het-word; die normally points at a de-word. When a sentence offers several nouns, the pointer's spelling eliminates most of them for free. A narrow exception for words denoting people appears below.

Step 1 On the page

Two sentences that differ by two letters. In an official letter that difference decides what you are being asked to sign.

In the style of a covering letter

Wij sturen u de brief over het formulier dat u moet ondertekenen.

Wij sturen u de brief over het formulier die u moet ondertekenen.

Both are ordinary Dutch. Both contain exactly the same nouns. In the first, dat can only be pointing at het formulier, so the form is what you sign. In the second, die can only be pointing at de brief, so the letter is. Nothing else in either sentence tells you which — not word order, not proximity, not sense. The whole distinction is carried by one word's spelling, and that spelling was determined by a property of the noun you were told to memorise for a different reason.

This is what gender is for, from a reading point of view. A speaker who gets it wrong sounds foreign. A reader who ignores it loses the ability to tell which of two things a sentence is talking about — and official Dutch produces sentences with four or five candidate nouns constantly.

Step 2 What it is doing

Two genders, unevenly split

Dutch nouns come in two classes, traditionally called common and neuter, and in practice you meet them as de-words and het-words. The split is lopsided. Across the A2 word list this book checks its examples against — 1044 nouns — 756 are de and 281 are het, with seven taking either. A little over seven in ten.

That ratio is itself useful. If you have to guess, guess de. But the point of this chapter is that for a reader you very often do not have to guess, because the text tells you.

The pointers

Four words do the pointing, and they come in matched pairs:

de-wordhet-word
This / these deze brief dit formulier
That / those die brief dat formulier
Pointing back (relative) de brief die … het formulier dat …

The bottom row is the one that pays. When a relative clause opens with dat, every de-word in the sentence is eliminated as a candidate, and roughly three-quarters of Dutch nouns are de-words. One word of spelling has thrown away most of the possibilities.

One human exception keeps the rule honest. In an expanding relative clause — the extra-information kind set off by commas — an het-word that denotes a person can sometimes take die by meaning rather than dat by grammatical gender:

Het kind, die inmiddels achttien is, ontvangt de brief.

The commas and the human referent are both doing work. Do not turn this into permission to attach die freely to any het-word. Treat it as a warning that the pointer is evidence, not an exceptionless decoder, when a neuter word names a person and the clause merely adds information about them.

Where the plural destroys it

Say this part out loud before you start relying on the device, because it is the limit and it arrives quickly. All plurals are de-words, whatever the singular was. Het formulier becomes de formulieren. So in the plural both classes collapse into one, and the pointer is always die:

Wij sturen u de brieven over de formulieren die u moet ondertekenen. The same sentence as step 1, in the plural. Die is now compatible with both nouns and resolves nothing at all. The minimal pair from step 1 cannot even be written here.

This is not a footnote. A letter listing several documents will very often list them in the plural, which is exactly the case where you most want the pointer and exactly the case where it has stopped working. When that happens you are back to sense and proximity, and you should know that you are.

And where a list destroys it too

One more case, because it is common in exactly the sentences this chapter is about. When two nouns are joined by en, they can act as a single plural thing, and then die may be pointing at both of them together rather than at either one:

Wij sturen u de aanslag en het formulier die u moet ondertekenen. Three readings, not two: the assessment, or — just as available — the assessment and the form as a pair. Die rules out the form on its own, and stops there.

So the honest statement of the rule is narrower than "the pointer identifies the noun." The pointer eliminates candidates; it does not always leave exactly one. Dat is the stronger of the two, because a coordination cannot be neuter singular — so dat really does force a single het-word. Die is weaker: it clears the neuter singulars out of the way and leaves you with the de-words, the plurals, and any coordination in the sentence.

What predicts gender, and what does not

Some of gender is rule-governed. Donaldson gives it six pages, and the rules that earn their keep at this level are these. Neuter (§7.1.2, printed 30–31):

  • All diminutives — anything ending -je: het kopje, het huisje, het briefje.
  • All infinitives used as nouns: het eten, het werken.
  • Nouns built from verb stems with be-, ge-, ont-, ver- — chapter 04's blocking prefixes again: het gesprek, het ontbijt, het verbod.
  • Words ending -um, -isme, -sel; colours; metals; names of countries and cities.

Common (§7.1.1, printed 27–29):

  • Words ending -ing or -ling: de rekening, de vergadering.
  • Words ending -heid, -nis, -teit, -ij, -ie, -tie.
  • Agent nouns and people; days, months and seasons; most one-syllable nouns made from verbs.

How well those rules actually hold

Because this book checks its Dutch against a machine-readable copy of that A2 word list, the rules can be tested rather than taken on trust. Counting every noun in the list:

-ing5757 denone
-tie1515 denone
-teit66 denone

-ij65 dehet schilderij

Two things are worth noticing. The first four are exceptionless in a thousand-word register, which is as close to a free rule as this language offers — and -je and -ing alone cover ninety-three nouns. The second is that the exceptions the count found are the exceptions Donaldson names: he prints de datum against the -um rule and het schilderij against -ij, and those are precisely the two words that turned up. A 1981 reference grammar and a 2009 course book's vocabulary list agreeing to the individual word is worth more than either alone.

And the part no rule covers

Now the honest half. For a large share of ordinary nouns, gender is simply a fact about the word, with nothing in its spelling or meaning to predict it. Donaldson says so directly in the same section. De brief but het formulier. De aanslag but het bedrag. De tafel but het bord. There is no rule; there is a list, and native speakers learned it as children.

That sounds discouraging and is not, because of what this chapter is for. You do not need to know a noun's gender in order to use the pointer. You need it only when you want to check a specific candidate — and then it is one dictionary lookup, and every Dutch dictionary prints it. The reading skill is noticing that the pointer is doing work at all.

Step 3 Read it

The minimal pair, worked

…het formulier die u moet ondertekenen

die → de aanslag

The pointer is a de-form, and the only de-word available is the assessment. You sign the assessment; the form is just enclosed.

…het formulier dat u moet ondertekenen

dat → het formulier

The pointer is a het-form, and only the form qualifies. You sign the form; the assessment is just enclosed.

Note what the reader who ignores gender does here: takes the nearest noun, which is het formulier, and is right half the time by accident.

The other dat

One collision is worth learning now, because it is common and because it looks identical. Dat is also the ordinary conjunction that, and then it points at nothing:

Read the clause and ask whether something is missing from it. That is the whole test, and it is about the clause rather than about the word in front of it.

het formulier dat u heeft ingevuld The clause is u heeft ___ ingevuld — filled in what? There is a hole where the object should be, and the noun in front fills it. Pointer.
Wij hebben gezien dat u het formulier niet volledig heeft ingevuld. The clause is u heeft het formulier niet volledig ingevuld — a complete statement with nothing missing. It is what was seen, not a description of a noun. Conjunction.

It is worth doing it this way rather than by looking at the preceding word, because the obvious shortcut is wrong. A noun can sit in front of a conjunction dat perfectly happily:

het feit dat u te laat bent A noun immediately before dat — and still a conjunction. The clause u bent te laat is complete; it does not describe which fact, it is the fact. Nouns like het feit, de reden and het idee take clauses this way routinely.

The gap test also generalises, which the shortcut would not have. Die works the same way, and so does everything chapter 06 adds: a modifier attached to a noun always has a hole in it where that noun belongs. Both kinds of clause send their verb to the end, so chapter 02 will not separate them — but chapter 02 does tell you where each clause stops, which is what you need in order to see what is inside it.

Three to try

For each die or dat, decide first whether it is pointing at all, and only then at what. Write both answers down before you open the worked versions. One of these three is not a pointer, and the whole value of the exercise is whether you caught it.

  1. Het bedrag dat u nog aan ons moet overmaken, staat onderaan de brief.
  2. De reden dat wij uw bezwaar afwijzen, staat in de bijlage.
  3. Het huisje dat u huurt, valt onder de regels die voor alle woningen gelden.
Show the worked answers

One. The clause is u nog aan ons moet overmaken — transfer what? There is a hole where the object belongs, so this is a pointer, and it is a het-form: het bedrag, the only candidate anyway. Worth doing because it is the ordinary case, where the pointer confirms what you had already assumed and costs nothing to check.

Two. This is the one the discarded shortcut would have failed. Dat sits immediately after a noun, de reden — and it is still a conjunction. Run the real test: wij uw bezwaar afwijzen is complete, with nothing missing from it. The clause is not telling you which reason; it is the reason. Note also that reden is a de-word, so a pointer here would have had to be die — a second, independent signal that this dat is not pointing at anything.

Three. Two pointers, and each demonstrates a different rule. Dat points at het huisje — a diminutive, so neuter by the exceptionless rule, whatever het huis was doing. Then die points at de regels, and here it is forced: regels is plural, and all plurals take die. The second pointer carries no gender information at all, and if there had been another plural noun in front of it you would have had to work out the reference some other way.

Did it work?

You have decided that a word is a pointer and named what it points at. Four tests, and the last one is free — the letter itself supplies the answer key.

  • The gap. Read the clause on its own. There must be a hole in it where your candidate noun belongs. If the clause is complete — wij uw bezwaar afwijzen — the word is a conjunction and points at nothing, however convincingly a noun sits in front of it.
  • Agreement. Dat can only point at a singular het-word. If your candidate takes de, either it is the wrong candidate or you are not looking at a pointer. This test cannot confirm an answer, but it rules one out for certain.
  • Removal. Take the whole clause out. What is left must still be a complete sentence, and it must still be about the same thing. If removing it breaks the main clause, you have cut in the wrong place — most often you stopped at the wrong verb, which is chapter 02's job, not this one's.
  • The article checks your chapter-01 split, for nothing. A compound takes the gender of its last element, so the article printed in the letter is a free test of how you decomposed the word. If you split inkomstenbelasting with belasting as the head, the letter must say de inkomstenbelasting — and it does. Where the printed article contradicts the head you chose, the split is wrong and the letter has just told you so. This is the first test in the book where an authentic document grades your work.

Where this procedure cannot decide

The pointer eliminates candidates. It does not select one, and there are two ordinary situations in which it eliminates nothing at all.

Two nouns of the same gender. In de brief over de beslissing die u heeft ontvangen, both brief and beslissing are de-words, so die is compatible with both. Did you receive the letter or the decision? The spelling is silent. Dutch resolves this the way English does — by sense, by which reading is plausible, and sometimes not at all.

Anything plural. All plurals take de and therefore die, whatever gender the singular had. A die after a plural noun has told you nothing whatever: it was forced, and it would have been spelled that way regardless. Two plural nouns in a row and you are reading unaided.

The right move in both cases is to notice that the mechanical test has run out, and say so. A reader who knows the pointer is uninformative here will look for other evidence; a reader who does not will take the pointer as confirmation of whichever noun they had in mind. That second reader is worse off than one who never learned the rule, which is the only way a correct rule can leave you further from the letter.

Step 4 In your own post

Find a sentence in a letter that mentions more than one document — an assessment and an annexe, a decision and a form, a letter and an attachment. These sentences are everywhere, because official writing lists things. Then find the die or dat that follows, and check which noun it can legally attach to before you decide which one it means.

Two situations repay the check most, and they are the two where a wrong answer costs something. A sentence that says what you must sign, return or pay — those almost always carry a relative clause specifying which document or which amount. And a sentence stating a deadline, where the clause is doing the work of saying which thing the date applies to.

One habit is enough: when you see dat after a noun, stop and notice that three-quarters of the sentence's nouns have just been ruled out. It is the cheapest piece of disambiguation in the language, and you have been reading past it for years.

Run the same four tests from step 3, and run the fourth one across the whole letter rather than only where a pointer appears. Every de or het in front of a long noun is a free verdict on a chapter-01 split, and a letter of two pages will hand you a dozen of them without being asked.

Then record what stayed open, and be strict about it. If the pointer was die and more than one de-word or any plural precedes it, write down that the pointer did not decide — not the reading you favour. The same goes for a clause where the gap test was unclear because you could not tell where the clause ended. Those two notes are the useful output of this exercise: the sentences where gender helped are ones you would probably have read correctly anyway, and the sentences where it did not are the ones worth taking to somebody who knows.

References

Donaldson's gender rules are §7.1, beginning at printed page 27: common-gender nouns at §7.1.1 (printed 27–29), neuter at §7.1.2 (printed 30–31), and the compound rule chapter 01 used at §7.1.3 (printed 32). He states in the same section that many nouns fit no rule and must be learned individually, which is why this chapter is built on the pointer rather than on the noun.

The percentages and the exception counts above were computed over the gender register in this book's repository — a machine-readable transcription of De Opmaat's A2 word list, which marks de or het on every noun. It is the same file the checks use to verify every article printed in these chapters. Two of its entries are wrong and are corrected there rather than copied; the corrections are recorded with their reasons, because a register that feeds a checker cannot be trusted without being tested.

Taaltalent deel 1 introduces nouns and articles early, and deel 2 covers deze, dit, die and dat as demonstratives. What neither course does at this level is connect the two — treat the article you learned with the noun as the key that later resolves a reference. That connection is the chapter.

Kaart

Nieuw

  • Gender is a pointer tag: die for de-words, dat for het-words
  • In an expanding clause, an het-word denoting a person can sometimes take die by meaning
  • deze/dit and die/dat as demonstratives
  • Seven nouns in ten are de, so dat eliminates most candidates
  • All plurals are de, so the pointer stops working in the plural
  • All diminutives are het — but de huisjes in the plural
  • Exceptionless endings: -je, -ing, -tie, -teit
  • Relative dat follows a noun; conjunction dat follows a verb
  • For many nouns no rule applies and gender is simply lexical

Terug

  • Which element of a compound decides its article — and what does that let the letter check for you? (ch. 01)

    The last one. So the article printed in front of a compound is a free verdict on how you split it: disagreement means the split was wrong.

  • Why do you need to know where a clause stops before you can run the gap test? (ch. 02)

    Because the gap test asks what is missing inside the clause, and you cannot see what is inside it until you know where it ends. Both relative clauses and dat-clauses send their verb to the end, so the verb's position will not separate them.

  • Where have you seen be-, ge-, ont- and ver- before, and what did they do there? (ch. 04)

    They are four of the six inseparable prefixes, and they blocked the participial ge-. Here the same four letters-strings predict a neuter noun. Same list, unrelated job.

Nog niet

  • Modifiers placed before the noun instead of after (ch. 06)
  • waarvan, waarbij, waarin — pointing with a preposition attached (ch. 07)
