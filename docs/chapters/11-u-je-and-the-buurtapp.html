Part IV · Register — Chapter 11

U, je, and the buurtapp

Ten chapters have helped you recover what a text says. This one asks what the way it is written tells you on top of that — and, just as importantly, about the things it looks like it tells you and does not.

Before you start (ch. 10)

You meet a joining word you have never seen. How do you find out what job it is doing?

Answer

Look at the verb in the clause beside it. At the end of its clause → subordinating, so that clause is a qualification. Inverted with its subject → adverbial, so that clause is a consequence. Unchanged → co-ordinating, only joining. The word order classifies the word, which means you never needed the vocabulary.

The device Every text carries two things at once: its literal content — the fact, action or request — and the relationship created by the way that content is written. The relationship is built out of address forms, softeners, diminutives and sentence shape, and it tells you what situation you are in and what role the sender is performing. It does not tell you who they are, how they normally talk, or how urgent the matter is. Reading it as evidence about a person rather than about a situation is the mistake this chapter exists to prevent.
Scope. This is not a list of formal words against informal ones. It is a way of reading the packaging around the literal content you have already extracted with chapters 08 to 10 — and of stating how much confidence the packaging actually supports.

Step 1 On the page

Two messages. The practical content is close to identical.

Gemeente · brief

Geachte heer, mevrouw,

Indien u gebruik wenst te maken van deze regeling, dient het formulier vóór maandag 12 mei te worden ingediend.

Buurtapp · bericht

Hoi! Kun je het formuliertje maandag even opsturen? Anders lukt het niet meer, hoor.

Both say: get the form in by Monday. A reader who has done Part III can extract the literal content from either. What separates them is everything else, and the second is in some ways the harder of the two.

The first announces itself. Geachte heer, mevrouw, indien rather than als, dient … te rather than moet, and a passive with no actor. Four markers pointing the same way. You have met every one of them in chapters 08, 09 and 10, and the useful new observation is that they arrived together.

The second contains three words that a dictionary will not help with. Formuliertje need not be a physically small form. Even does not mean the job is quick. And hoor at the end of a sentence has no stable translation at all. Each is doing something to the relationship rather than to the literal content, and a reader who skips them loses the entire difference between the two messages.

Here is the trap, and it is worth naming before the method. The friendly message contains a harder deadline than the official one. Anders lukt het niet meer — otherwise it will not work any more — is a stated consequence, which chapter 10 taught you settles the question. The formal letter states a date and no consequence at all. Read for tone, and you would rank them the other way round.

Step 2 What it is doing

What register is evidence about

Register is strong evidence about three things and weak evidence about a fourth, and keeping them apart is most of the skill.

Register is good evidence about…and poor evidence about…
the genre: legal decision, commercial mail, neighbourhood message the sender as a person — how they normally speak, whether they are friendly, whether they know you
the role the sender is performing in this exchange: authority, supplier, neighbour, peer
the relationship the text constructs: distant, procedural, conciliatory

The reason for the right-hand column is that the same sender changes register by situation. A gemeente writes u and indien in a decision and je in a recycling-app notification, on the same day, about the same household. A company uses your first name and je because a style guide told it to. What you are reading is the role being performed, not the person performing it.

The address forms, and why they are less informative than they look

The system itself is simple and stable. U is the distant form, subject and object alike, with uw as its possessive. Je is the close form and also the unstressed one; jij is the same pronoun when it carries emphasis, and jouw is the possessive. Jullie is the plural.

What is not stable is what choosing one of them means, and this is the place in the book where the age of a source matters most. Donaldson's account is careful, detailed and describes social conventions that have changed substantially since 1981: he records that a sizeable minority still say u to their parents, that schoolchildren must say u to teachers, and that U and Uw are often capitalised in very formal letters though the tradition is waning. It was waning in 1981. Capitalised U and Uw are now uncommon outside deliberately traditional or ceremonial writing.

That is not a defect in the grammar; it is the nature of the thing being described. The forms are stable and the conventions attached to them move, which is exactly why register has to be read as evidence about a situation rather than looked up in a table.

The devices that carry little literal content

Three common devices do relationship work while contributing almost nothing to the literal content. They are the ones a dictionary fails on, and they are extremely common in the informal written Dutch a reader actually receives.

  • The diminutive. -tje and its variants do mark small size — but that is not their only job, and often not the one in play. Een formuliertje need not be a physically small form: here the diminutive presents the form, or the request involving it, as familiar and manageable — a small imposition rather than a small object. Donaldson records two further directions it can go: on adverbs its function is toning down the intensity of meaning — zachtjes, netjes, eventjes — and on nouns it can carry a derogatory connotation, as in een raar taaltje for a strange lingo. Softening and belittling are related effects of the same form, and the sentence around it decides which is active.
  • Softeners. Even presents an action as brief and easy. It is a claim about how the request is being framed, not about the task — the diminutive eventjes, which Donaldson glosses as just a minute, is the same move made twice.
  • Sentence shape. A question is the commonest way informal Dutch packages an instruction. Kun je het even opsturen? is not asking about your ability, which chapter 08 established for kunt u and which holds identically here.

And one that resists a gloss entirely. Sentence-final hoor presents the statement as something the reader is expected to accept or already recognise. What that does depends on the sentence it is attached to: it can reassure, soften, insist, or sharpen. It has no stable one-word English translation, and — this is the part that matters — it does not reliably mean the message is friendly.

Anders lukt het niet meer, hoor. Attached to a hard consequence, this hoor is far more likely to be underlining that the consequence is real than to be softening it. The particle in step 1's message is not making the deadline gentler.

This project's shelf has no source that treats the sentence-final particles as a class, so the account above is from observation and is marked accordingly. What is sourced is the diminutive's toning-down function, printed 48. The reason for the caution is not modesty: a particle that can both soften and sharpen is exactly the kind of evidence this chapter warns against reading as a fixed label.

Step 3 Read it

The procedure

  1. Identify the setting. Letter, app message, work email, commercial notification, neighbourhood chat. This is usually free — you know where you found it.
  2. Collect a cluster of markers. Address form, greeting, the chapter-10 connectives, whether obligations use dient te or moet, whether actors are named, diminutives, softeners. Never decide from u or je alone.
  3. Infer the role, not the identity. Institution to resident, company to customer, neighbour to neighbour.
  4. Separate the literal content from the packaging. What action is literally required, and how is it softened, distanced or emphasised? These are two separate lines and they can point in opposite directions.
  5. State your confidence and your evidence. Formal, informal, mixed or unclear — and on the strength of what.

Applied to step 1's second message:

Registerinformal
EvidenceHoi, je, diminutive, even, hoor, question form — six markers, all agreeing
Sender roleneighbour or group member — probable, from the setting
Literal actionsend the form on Monday
Interpersonal forcesoftened request
Urgencyhigh — but from anders lukt het niet meer, not from the register

The last two lines are the point of the whole exercise. The interpersonal force and the urgency come apart. This is a softened request carrying a hard consequence, and the softening is doing nothing to the deadline. Chapter 10's consequence test is what establishes the urgency; the register establishes only how politely it is being delivered.

You can stop here and use the procedure. Setting, cluster, role, literal content against packaging, confidence. Everything below is the rules that look right and are not, the case this cannot settle, and where to practise.

Three to try

For each, give register, evidence, sender role, literal action and interpersonal force — and say what the register does not establish. Write it down first. One of the three is designed so that the obvious inference from the address form is wrong.

  1. Hallo Anna, leuk dat je klant bij ons bent! Wil je even je gegevens controleren? Dat scheelt gedoe later.
  2. Zou u zo vriendelijk willen zijn de fiets niet voor de deur te zetten?
  3. Beste bewoner, wij vragen u vriendelijk het grofvuil pas op de ophaaldag buiten te zetten. Anders krijgen wij een boete.
Show the worked answers

One. This is the one the address form gets wrong. Hallo plus a first name plus je plus even reads as a message from someone who knows you. Klant bij ons settles it: this is a company writing to a customer, and the informality is a house style. Register: informal. Sender role: commercial, to a customer. Literal action: check your details. Interpersonal force: softened, and framed as being for your benefit. What the register does not establish: whether this is required at all — and wil je with no consequence stated suggests it is not.

Two. Zou u zo vriendelijk willen zijn … is elaborately polite, and elaborate politeness in Dutch, as in English, often marks friction rather than warmth. Register: formal. Sender role: unclear — and this is the honest answer, because a neighbour who has asked twice already writes exactly like this. Literal action: do not put the bicycle in front of the door. Interpersonal force: a request in form, a complaint in effect. What the register does not establish: any authority whatever. Nobody here can make you do anything.

Three. Beste bewoner — addressed to a role, not a name — with u, vriendelijk and a plain wij vragen. Register: formal but not official: no dient te, no indien, and the actor is named as wij rather than hidden, which by chapter 09 is atypical of a public body writing a decision. Sender role: probably a housing association or building manager, not a municipality. And the consequence gives it away completely — anders krijgen wij een boete. The penalty falls on them. A body with authority over you states a consequence that falls on you.

Did it work?

Six tests, and every one of them is a rule that looks true and is not. They are listed as traps rather than as instructions because that is the shape the mistakes actually take.

  • U does not prove an institution. It marks distance, unfamiliarity, respect or caution. A stranger, a much older neighbour and a shop all use it.
  • Je does not prove friendship. Organisations and advertisers use it deliberately. If your only evidence is the pronoun, you have one marker and the procedure asks for a cluster.
  • Informal wording does not make a request optional, and formal wording does not confer authority. Step 1's friendly message carried the harder deadline. Exercise two was formal and its sender could compel nothing. Force comes from chapters 08 and 10 — the obligation verb and the stated consequence — never from tone.
  • A diminutive is not necessarily about physical size. It can also soften, familiarise or belittle. Decide which from the sentence around it, not from the ending.
  • Even does not mean the task is quick. It means the sender is presenting it as quick. Those are different claims and only one of them is about the world.
  • Register does not establish urgency. Fill the Urgency line from the deadline and the consequence, and if you wrote it down from the tone, write it again.

Where this procedure cannot decide

The procedure assumes a text has a register. A great deal of what now arrives does not.

Verzekeraar · e-mail

Hoi Anna, even een berichtje over je polis!

De verzekeringnemer dient wijzigingen binnen 30 dagen schriftelijk door te geven, bij gebreke waarvan geen dekking bestaat.

Vragen? Laat het ons weten!

Three registers in four lines. A friendly greeting with a diminutive and a softener; a sentence of contract language with dient te, a nominalisation, no actor and a waarvan construction from chapter 07; then a cheerful sign-off. Which role is this sender performing? Genuinely both. A marketing template has been wrapped around a clause somebody's legal department wrote, and neither layer is a pose — they are simply different parts of the same organisation, sent as one message.

There is no reading of the whole that is correct. The procedure has to be run paragraph by paragraph, and the honest record is that the message has a friendly frame and a binding middle. The middle is the message.

This case is not an oddity. It is a common shape of contemporary commercial and institutional mail, and it is the strongest practical reason for the rule at the top of this chapter: register tells you about the situation and the role, and a single text can contain more than one of each.

Step 4 In your own post

This week's exercise needs two documents rather than one. Take an official letter and a buurtapp or WhatsApp message, and run the procedure on both. The contrast is the lesson; either alone teaches much less.

On the formal document, count the markers rather than judging the tone: u, Geachte, indien, dient te, passives without actors, nominalisations. Six chapters of this book are a checklist for that list, and seeing them arrive together is the point.

On the informal one, find the devices that carry little literal content — diminutives, even, maar, hoor, toch, question marks on instructions. Then delete them mentally and read what is left. What remains is the literal content, and it is usually blunter than the message felt.

Run the same six tests from step 3 on both, and the third one on every document without exception — informal wording does not make a request optional, and formal wording does not confer authority. That test is the one this chapter exists for.

Then record what stayed open, and there will be two kinds. Any document with mixed register, where the frame and the middle disagree — note which paragraph carries the binding language, because that is the one that matters. And any sender role you could only mark probable. A housing association and a municipality write similarly and can do very different things to you, and exercise three showed the tell: look at whom the stated consequence falls on.

Part IV ends here

Two chapters, one question: what does the way this is written tell me, beyond what it says? Chapter 10 gave you the words that switch an obligation on and off and state its price, and showed that their word order classifies them so you need not know them. This chapter gave you the packaging around all of it, and a firm limit on what the packaging proves.

Everything the book teaches is now in place. Part V does not add machinery: chapter 12 clears the last obstacles that are pure decoding — abbreviations, dates, amounts — chapter 13 is about where in a letter each of these things sits, and chapter 14 reads one letter from the top with all of it at once.

References

The pronoun forms are Donaldson §8.1, printed pages 50–57: u and uw, je as the unemphatic form against jij as the emphatic one, jullie as the plural, and men being replaced by je in general speech — which is the same men chapter 09 met as an actorless subject.

This chapter is where this book's standing caution about its main source is not a footnote but the subject. Donaldson at printed 52–53 records that a sizeable minority say u to their parents, that schoolchildren must say u to teachers, and that capitalised U in formal letters is a waning tradition. That is an accurate description of 1981 and it is not a description of the post arriving now. The forms he gives are correct and unchanged; the social conventions he attaches to them have moved, which is the general shape of the problem and the reason this chapter treats register as situational evidence rather than as a lookup table.

The diminutive material is §7.5, printed 43–49. Two facts from printed 48 do real work here: that on adverbs the diminutive's function is toning down the intensity of meaning, with eventjes glossed as just a minute; and that on nouns it can carry a derogatory connotation. Those two together are why the chapter says size is not the diminutive's only job. That all diminutives are neuter is §7.1.2, printed 30, which chapter 05 already used.

The sentence-final particles are the gap. This project's shelf has no source that treats hoor, maar and their relatives as a class, and the passage describing them is marked accordingly rather than presented as sourced. De Opmaat and Taaltalent both teach u against je as a politeness choice, which is right for a speaker and incomplete for a reader: neither treats register as evidence to be weighed, because neither is written for someone trying to work out what a document is.

Kaart

Nieuw

  • Register is evidence about the situation and the role — genre, sender's role, the relationship the text constructs
  • It is poor evidence about the sender as a person, and no evidence at all about urgency
  • u / uw, je (unemphatic) / jij (emphatic) / jouw, jullie
  • Decide from a cluster, never from the pronoun alone
  • The diminutive softens or belittles — it is not about size
  • Even frames a task as quick; it does not make it quick
  • A question can be an instruction — chapter 08's kunt u, in the informal register
  • Force comes from the obligation verb and the stated consequence, never from tone
  • Mixed register is normal: run the procedure paragraph by paragraph

Terug

  • Which small word marks an obligation as belonging to the formal written register? (ch. 08)

    te, before the infinitive that dienen governs. It is one marker of a cluster, not a verdict on its own.

  • A formal letter states an obligation and names nobody. How confident may you be about who must act? (ch. 09)

    Only as confident as the evidence: stated, recoverable, probable or not stated. The same discipline applies to the sender's role in this chapter, and for the same reason.

  • What turns an ambiguous requirement into a settled one — and is tone one of the things that can do it? (ch. 08, 10)

    A stated consequence or a legal basis. Tone is not one of them: a softened request can carry a hard consequence, and a formal one can carry none.

  • Why does indien tell you something even though it means exactly what als means? (ch. 10)

    Because the choice is not about meaning. It is a register marker, and it travels with the others — which is what makes a cluster readable.
