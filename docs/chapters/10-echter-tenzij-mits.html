Part IV · Register — Chapter 10

Echter, tenzij, mits

Part III found the obligation and worked out whose it was. It could not tell you whether the obligation applies to you at all. That is carried by a handful of short words, and you do not need to know any of them to read it — the word order does the work.

Before you start (ch. 09)

In de beoordeling van uw aanvraag, what does van mark — and what would mark the actor?

Answer

Van marks the thing acted on: the application is what gets assessed. The actor, if the sentence names one, comes with door — de beoordeling door de gemeente. Reading the van phrase as the actor turns the sentence inside out.

The device A joining word does one of three jobs to the obligation next to it: it switches it on under a condition, switches it off under an exception, or states what follows if it is ignored. Dutch tells you which by moving the verb. A word that sends the following verb to the end of its clause is attaching a condition; a word that makes subject and verb swap places is stating a consequence; a word that changes nothing is only joining. Read the verb, not the vocabulary.
Where this sits. This chapter completes the job Part III started. Chapter 08 found the obligation, chapter 09 found — or honestly refused to invent — the actor, and neither could say whether the requirement is live. It opens Part IV because the words that do this are also the clearest marker of formal register in the language, which is chapter 11's subject.

Step 1 On the page

Four sentences. Each contains an obligation you could already extract, and each is modified by one short word.

Betreft: betalingsherinnering

U dient het bedrag vóór 1 juni te betalen, tenzij u bezwaar heeft gemaakt.

Uitstel is mogelijk, mits u dit schriftelijk aanvraagt.

Wij kunnen uw verzoek echter niet honoreren.

Betaal op tijd, anders wordt de vordering verhoogd.

The first two are the ones that cost money. Tenzij and mits are five letters and four letters, they sit in the middle of the sentence, and a reader working hard on the obligation itself will pass over both. They point in opposite directions. One says the requirement stands unless something is true; the other says it stands only if something is true — and a reader who takes them for the same kind of small linking word has a fifty per cent chance of doing the wrong thing.

The third stops the eye somewhere unexpected. Echter means however, and an English reader looks for however at the start of a sentence. Here it is the fifth word, wedged between the object and the negative, which is where Dutch routinely puts it. A word doing the work of a paragraph-level contrast is sitting in the middle of a clause looking like an adverb of manner.

The fourth is the one chapter 08 promised you. When an affirmative moeten could be a requirement or merely advice, the thing that settles it is a stated consequence — and anders, otherwise, is the commonest way a Dutch letter states one.

None of these four words is hard. They are short, they are common, and every one of them is in a beginner's vocabulary list. What they are not is noticed, because nothing about them announces that they are carrying the logic of the paragraph.

Step 2 What it is doing

Three classes, told apart by the verb

Dutch joining words fall into three groups, and the grouping is not about meaning. It is about what happens to the word order of the clause that follows — which means you can classify a word you have never seen.

ClassEffect on the next clauseMembers you will meet
Co-ordinating nothing — ordinary main-clause order en, maar, want, of
Subordinating the finite verb goes to the end of its clause indien, mits, tenzij, als, wanneer, omdat, zodat
Adverbial inversion — subject and verb swap, so the verb stays second anders, daarom, toch, desondanks, integendeel

The middle row is chapter 02's rule arriving with a name. You already knew that omdat sends the verb to the end; what you did not know is that this is a class and that its membership is exactly the set of words that attach a condition, a reason or a purpose. A verb at the end of its clause means that clause is a qualification of another one — never the main statement.

Condition — verb at the end

…, mits u dit schriftelijk aanvraagt.

Subordinating. This clause qualifies; something else is the main claim.

Consequence — verb second, inverted

…, anders wordt de vordering verhoogd.

Adverbial. Anders takes first position, so the verb follows it and the subject comes after. This clause is a main statement about what will happen.

The pair that inverts the instruction

Mits and tenzij are the same operation in opposite polarity, and both are ordinary in official Dutch and absent from ordinary speech.

mits provided that — the obligation or permission holds only if this is true.
tenzij unless — the obligation holds except when this is true.

Set against one sentence they are plainly opposite:

U hoeft niet te betalen, mits u bezwaar heeft gemaakt. You need not pay — but only if you have objected. No objection, and you pay.
U dient te betalen, tenzij u bezwaar heeft gemaakt. You must pay — except if you have objected. No objection, and you pay. The same outcome, arrived at from the other side.

Those two happen to agree, which is exactly why the pair is dangerous: on many sentences they will not, and nothing about the words themselves warns you. The reliable move is to rewrite the sentence in English with only if or unless and check the result against what the letter is plainly for.

Indien, and the register signal

Indien is simply if. It is subordinating like als, means the same as als, and differs only in being literary. That makes it useless as vocabulary and useful as a marker: a letter that writes indien rather than als is a letter that will also write dient u te rather than u moet, and will hide its actors. Chapter 11 is about reading that signal deliberately.

Echter, and where it hides

Echter means however and marks a contrast with what came before — very often the contrast between what you asked for and what you are getting. Its peculiarity for a reader is positional: unlike English however, it does not need to stand at the front. It frequently sits inside the clause, after the subject or after the object, where an English reader is not looking for a discourse word.

Wij kunnen uw verzoek echter niet honoreren. "We cannot, however, grant your request." The however is the fifth word and it governs the whole sentence — and the sentence is a refusal.

Donaldson lists the adverbial conjunctions and echter is not among them, so its behaviour is described here from observation rather than from a source in this project's shelf. What is sourced is the class it belongs to and how that class behaves. Treat the positional claim as reliable for reading and unproven here.

The practical point stands whatever its classification. When a paragraph of a letter turns against you, the word doing the turning may be in the middle of a sentence. Reading only the first word of each sentence for connectives will miss it.

Step 3 Read it

The procedure

  1. Extract the obligation first — status, actor, action, deadline (ch. 08, 09). This chapter modifies that result; it does not replace it.
  2. Find the joining word, and do not assume it is at the start of a sentence.
  3. Look at the verb in the clause beside it. At the end of its clause → subordinating, and that clause is a qualification. Inverted with the subject → adverbial, and that clause is a consequence. Unchanged → co-ordinating, and it is only joining.
  4. Ask which of the three jobs it is doing to the obligation: switching it on under a condition, switching it off under an exception, or stating what follows from ignoring it.
  5. Rewrite the obligation with the qualification attached, in one English sentence. If you cannot, you have not finished.

Applied to step 1's first sentence. The obligation is already known: status required, actor u, action pay the amount, deadline 1 June. The joining word is tenzij, and the verb in its clause — gemaakt, with heeft before it — sits at the end. So it is subordinating, and the clause qualifies. Which job? It switches the obligation off in a stated case. Rewritten:

You must pay the amount before 1 June — unless you have lodged an objection.

And now the extraction changes. The Status line is no longer simply required; it is required, conditional on not having objected. That is a different instruction, and it is one a reader who stopped at chapter 08 would have got wrong.

You can stop here and use the procedure. Find the joining word, look at where the verb went, decide which of three jobs it is doing, and rewrite the obligation with the qualification attached. Everything below is the ways it misfires, the case it cannot settle, and where these words sit in a letter.

Three to try

For each, name the class from the word order, say which of the three jobs the word is doing, and rewrite the obligation in one English sentence. Write it down before opening the answers. One of the three contains a word that takes either word order, so the word alone will not tell you.

  1. Indien u het niet eens bent met dit besluit, kunt u binnen zes weken bezwaar maken.
  2. De termijn is verstreken, dus kunt u geen bezwaar meer maken.
  3. Wij verlenen uitstel, mits het verschuldigde bedrag binnen veertien dagen wordt voldaan.
Show the worked answers

One. Indien, and the verb of its clause — bent — is at the end, so subordinating. Job: it switches something on under a condition. But look at what it switches on: kunt u, which by chapter 08 is availability, not obligation. Status: optional, conditional on your disagreeing. The deadline of six weeks attaches to the option, not to a requirement. Note also that the subordinate clause comes first, so the main clause inverts — kunt u and not u kunt — which is chapter 02's rule and a second confirmation that the first clause was subordinate.

Two. This is the one where the word does not decide. Dus takes either construction. Here the verb and subject have inverted — kunt u — so it is behaving adverbially and the clause is a consequence. Written the other way, dus u kunt geen bezwaar meer maken, it would be co-ordinating and mean the same thing. The word order is the evidence and the word is not. Job: stating what follows. Status: not required, and the content is that a right has lapsed — which chapter 09 taught you to read in is verstreken.

Three. Mits, verb at the end (voldaan, with wordt before it), so subordinating and switching on. Rewritten: we grant a postponement, provided the amount owed is paid within fourteen days. Two things to notice. The condition is in the passive with no actor — chapter 09 — and the Actor line for the paying is recoverable, since a letter granting you a postponement is not proposing to pay itself. And the whole sentence is a grant that becomes a requirement: the postponement is real, and it has a price with a deadline of its own.

Did it work?

You have classified a joining word and reattached it to an obligation. Five tests, and the first three can each show the reading is wrong.

  • The word order must match the class you assigned. If you called a word subordinating, the finite verb in its clause is at the end. If you called it adverbial, subject and verb have swapped. If neither is true, the classification is wrong — and the word order is the evidence, not your memory of the word.
  • Restate mits as only if and tenzij as unless, and check the result makes sense as a letter. These two invert each other. If your reading has an institution granting something unconditionally, or demanding something in a case where it plainly would not, you have them the wrong way round.
  • A consequence clause is not an obligation on you. Anders wordt de vordering verhoogd tells you what the office will do. It belongs on the Status line as the price of non-compliance, not as a second action for you to perform.
  • Some words take either construction. Dus and alleen can be co-ordinating or adverbial, and als and of each cover two different jobs — chapter 02 flagged of as both or and whether. For these, the word order decides and the vocabulary does not.
  • Rewrite it as one English sentence. If the qualification will not attach — if you cannot say what it is a condition on — you have found the unresolved case below, and that is a result rather than a failure.

Where this procedure cannot decide

The procedure tells you what a condition is. It does not tell you how much of the sentence it covers, and Dutch marks that no better than English does.

U dient het formulier en de bijlage in te sturen, tenzij u al staat ingeschreven.

Does the exception release you from sending both, or only from sending the annexe? The grammar is silent. Both readings are available and they differ by one document — which, in a process where an incomplete submission is returned unprocessed, is the difference between finishing and starting again.

English has the same problem and solves it the same way, badly: by position, by comma placement, and by what the reader assumes the institution meant. Dutch does not have a device that fixes the scope, so no amount of word-order analysis will settle it.

Two things narrow it in practice. A condition placed before the obligation — Tenzij u al staat ingeschreven, dient u … — takes wide scope much more reliably, because it is stated before there is anything to attach narrowly to. And a repeated element in the condition (tenzij u de bijlage al heeft ingestuurd) names its own target. Where neither is present, record both readings and act on the wider one — sending a document you did not have to send costs a stamp, and the other error costs the application.

Step 4 In your own post

Go back to the ledger. For every row where the Status line says required, check whether a condition is attached to it — and the check is a search for six words: indien, als, tenzij, mits, wanneer, voorzover. A hit means the Status line was incomplete.

Then a second search, for the consequence: anders, zodat, daarom, indien niet, bij niet-tijdige. These tell you the price, and the price is what turns chapter 08's undecided moeten — requirement or advice — into a settled reading. A stated consequence is a requirement.

Where they cluster is worth knowing, and it is not where you would expect. Conditions sit in the paragraph after the decision, not with the instruction — a letter states what it has decided, then states the requirement, and only then qualifies it. And the consequence is usually in the last two sentences of a paragraph, often in a sentence short enough to look like a formality.

Run the same five tests from step 3 on every hit, and the second one on every mits and tenzij without exception. They are the two words in this book where a confident misreading produces a coherent, plausible, exactly wrong instruction.

Then record what stayed open, and there will be one kind. Any condition whose scope you could not fix — where the exception might cover the whole obligation or only part of it. Mark both readings, and note which one you acted on. That is the only place in this book where the recommended response to an unresolved reading is to do the more expensive thing anyway.

References

Donaldson's conjunctions chapter is §12, printed pages 190–204, and he opens it by saying that the distinction between conjunctions and certain adverbs is sometimes difficult to make and that he will therefore treat together all the words that join clauses, whatever their classification. This chapter takes the same view for the same reason: the reader needs the function, not the label.

The three classes and their word-order signatures are his. Co-ordinating conjunctions have no effect on the word-order of the following clause, §12.1 at printed 190. Subordinating conjunctions send the verb of the dependent clause to the end of that clause, §12.2 at printed 192. Adverbial conjunctions are adverbs functioning as conjunctions, and their adverbial quality is obvious from the word-order which follows, i.e. inversion of subject and verb, §12.3 at printed 199.

The list at printed 193 is where tenzij (unless), mits (provided that) and indien come from, the last marked as literary — which is the basis for reading it as a register signal rather than as vocabulary. Anders is in the adverbial list at printed 199. That dus can be either co-ordinating or adverbial, with the word order deciding, is his note at printed 191, and exercise two is built on it.

One thing this chapter asserts without a source on the shelf. Echter does not appear in Donaldson's adverbial list, or anywhere else in the conjunctions chapter. Its class is described here by analogy with the words he does list, and the claim about where it sits in the clause is from observation. The chapter marks that passage rather than quietly presenting it as sourced, which is the same standard applied to every other page: a claim is checked, or it says it is not.

Neither course reaches these words as a set. Taaltalent 2 teaches omdat and als, which is the right thing for a learner building sentences. Mits, tenzij, indien and voorzover belong to a register nobody speaks, and a reader meets all four in the first letter that grants something conditionally.

Kaart

Nieuw

  • Three classes, told apart by the verb: co-ordinating changes nothing, subordinating sends the verb to the end, adverbial inverts subject and verb
  • The class tells you the job: qualification, or consequence, or mere joining
  • mits = only if; tenzij = unless. Opposite polarity, and swapping them inverts the instruction
  • indien is als in a formal coat — useless as vocabulary, useful as a register signal
  • anders states the price of non-compliance — and a stated consequence settles chapter 08's moeten
  • echter often sits inside the clause, not at the front
  • dus takes either construction, so the word order decides and the word does not
  • Scope is not marked: a condition may cover the whole obligation or only part

Terug

  • What happens to the finite verb in a clause introduced by omdat — and why does that matter here? (ch. 02)

    It goes to the end. That is the signature of the whole subordinating class, so the position of the verb classifies a joining word you have never seen.

  • An affirmative u moet could be a requirement or advice. What settles it? (ch. 08)

    A stated consequence or a legal basis nearby. This chapter supplies the consequence words — anders, zodat, indien niet.

  • A condition is stated in the passive with no actor. How confident may you be about who acts? (ch. 09)

    Only as confident as the evidence: stated, recoverable, probable or not stated. A condition on you is usually recoverable, because the letter addresses u — but that is an inference and should be recorded as one.

  • Which small word tells you an obligation is in the formal register rather than the spoken one? (ch. 08)

    te, before the infinitive dienen governs. A letter writing dient u te is the same letter that writes indien.
