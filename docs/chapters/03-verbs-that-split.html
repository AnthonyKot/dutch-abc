Verbs that split

Chapter 02 sent you to the end of the clause and something was waiting there that did not look like a verb. It is a verb. It is the other half of the one you found at the front, and neither half means what the joined word means.

Before you start (ch. 02)

In Wij hebben uw bezwaar afgewezen omdat u het formulier niet op tijd heeft ingediend, which verb belongs to the main clause — and what marks the boundary?

Answer

afgewezen, in the middle of the line, not ingediend at the end. The boundary is marked by omdat and by nothing else — there is no comma. Walking to the end of the sentence gives you the wrong clause's verb.

The device A large class of Dutch verbs is built from a short particle plus a verb, and the two come apart. When the verb is finite in a main clause, the particle is left behind at the end of the clause — ten or fifteen words away. Looked up separately, both halves mislead. Looked up joined, the word is in every dictionary.

Step 1 On the page

The first sentence is the one chapter 02 left unfinished. The second is its close relative, and both are the kind of line that decides whether money arrives.

In the style of an assessment letter

Wij maken het bedrag binnen 1 week na de datum van deze brief over op uw rekening.

De Belastingdienst betaalt het bedrag na de behandeling van uw bezwaar binnen vier weken aan u terug.

In the second sentence, thirteen words separate betaalt from terug. A reader who knows both words still has a problem, because the sentence is not about paying and it is not about something being behind or back. It is about a refund, and the word that says so has been cut in half.

This is the failure the chapter is named for, and it has a specific shape. You did not fail to recognise a word. You recognised two words correctly and got the sentence wrong anyway — which is worse, because nothing tells you to stop and check. Chapter 01's unknown compound at least announced itself as unknown.

Courses do teach this. Taaltalent covers separable verbs across two units in deel 1 and returns to separable versus inseparable in deel 2, both before A2. So this chapter is not an introduction to the grammar. It is about what to do when you meet the grammar at a distance of thirteen words, in a letter, with no exercise instructions telling you what is going on.

Step 2 What it is doing

The shape of the verb

A separable verb is a short particle welded to an ordinary verb: over + maken gives overmaken, to transfer. terug + betalen gives terugbetalen, to refund. That is how the dictionary lists it and that is how the infinitive is written: as one word.

In speech the particle carries the stress, which is what makes this class a class. You cannot hear that on a page, so it is offered here only as the reason the rule below has the shape it does — and as something to keep in reserve if you ever need to say one of these aloud.

Where the two halves go

The same verb appears in five shapes, depending on what the clause is doing with it. Only the second one splits at a distance, and it is the one letters use most.

SituationWrittenExample
Infinitive, after a modal joined, at the end Wij kunnen het bedrag pas na 1 juni overmaken.
Finite, main clause split Wij maken het bedrag na 1 juni over.
Finite, subordinate clause joined, at the end … omdat wij het bedrag pas na 1 juni overmaken.
With te split by te Het is niet nodig om het bedrag eerder over te maken.
Past participle ge- inserted Wij hebben het bedrag op 3 juni overgemaakt.

Two of those rows are worth pausing on. The te row catches readers out because it produces three separate words — over te maken — and the middle one is the most ordinary word in the language. If you meet te wedged between a short particle and an infinitive, join the outer two and read the whole thing as one verb.

The participle row is why overgemaakt has no entry of its own to find: the ge- has been inserted inside the word rather than put on the front, so the form on the page shares neither its opening nor its spelling with the infinitive you need. Chapter 04 is about how these forms are built and how to get back from one to its infinitive. For now, note only that this row is the reason a separable verb can defeat a dictionary even when it is written as a single word.

Which prefixes split, and which never do

Not every verb with a prefix is separable, and the difference is not decoration: it decides where the verb's second half is, or whether there is one. Six prefixes are inseparable and never come apart:

be- · er- · ge- · her- · ont- · ver-

So verrekenen, beoordelen and herstellen stay whole wherever they appear: Wij verrekenen het bedrag met uw voorlopige aanslag. Nothing is waiting at the end of that clause, and chapter 02's step 2 correctly returns nothing.

The particles that do split are the short, concrete ones — the words you already know as prepositions and adverbs. The ones that saturate official letters are:

aan · af · bij · door · in · mee · na · op · over · terug · toe · uit · vast · voor · weg

The meaning does not add up, and this is the point

Chapter 01 taught you to split a long noun and read it from the right, and the pieces added up: belasting plus jaar really is a tax year. Do not carry that expectation across. The structure is the same — the verb on the right is the head, the particle modifies it — but the meaning of a separable verb is frequently not the sum of its parts.

Adding up the halves

over + maken

Over, across, about — plus to make. Nothing here says transfer. At best you assemble something vague and move on.

The joined word

overmaken

To transfer money. One entry, unambiguous, in every dictionary. The halves were never going to get you there.

The same holds for doorgeven (to report, to pass on), aangeven (to declare), opgeven (to state — or to give up), indienen (to submit) and vaststellen (to determine, in the sense a tax office determines a figure). These are single lexical items that happen to be written in two pieces. Treat the particle as part of the spelling of the verb, not as a word in its own right.

When the particle is evidence

A handful of prefixes go both ways, and voor-, over-, door-, om- and onder- are the ones you will meet. The same spelling gives two different verbs with two different meanings, and in speech they are told apart by stress — which, again, is invisible here.

But a reader has something a listener does not: the split itself is the evidence. If the particle has been stranded at the end of the clause, you are looking at the separable verb. If it has not, you are looking at the inseparable one.

Deze fout komt in oudere aanslagen regelmatig voor. Split — komt … voor. The separable verb: this error occurs regularly.
Wij voorkomen deze fout met een extra controle. Not split. The inseparable verb: we prevent this error. Same five letters at the front, opposite kind of statement.

The honest limit: this test only works where the verb is finite in a main clause. In a subordinate clause, as an infinitive, or with te, both verbs are written as one word and the page tells you nothing. There you are back to context — and to noticing that occurring and preventing are rarely both plausible in the same sentence.

Step 3 Read it

The procedure, extended

Chapter 02 gave you three moves. This chapter adds one, in the middle:

  1. Find the finite verb. It is early.
  2. Go to the end of the clause.
  3. If what is waiting there is a short word that looks like a preposition, test the joined infinitive as a candidate. Carry it back to the finite verb, restore the infinitive, and look that form up — but do not trust the join until it passes the checks below.
  4. Read the verb, now whole, and then the middle.

Applied to the first sentence in step 1: the finite verb is maken; the end of the clause holds over; joined, that is overmaken, to transfer. The sentence says the amount will be transferred to your account within a week. Note what you did not have to do: work out what over was doing there, or decide which noun it belonged to.

Three things that are not the particle

The move above is reliable enough to build a habit on, but it over-applies in three ways, all visible in sentences you will meet outside this chapter.

The particle is not always the last word. Chapter 02's tail rule still holds: material can be pushed out past the verb. In Wij maken het bedrag … over op uw rekening, the particle is over and op uw rekening follows it. Two short prepositional-looking words in a row, and only the first belongs to the verb. Read the one that closes the packed middle of the clause, not the one nearest the full stop.

Not every stranded short word is a particle. Dutch has another construction that leaves a preposition marooned at the end of a clause, with its partner (er, daar, hier or waar) sitting much earlier. That is chapter 07, and the sign to look for is one of those four words already in the clause.

A short word can also close a direction or time phrase. De grens over, het huis uit and het hele jaar door are built around the noun phrase immediately before the short word. Grammars disagree about whether some movement cases are best analysed as a postposition or as part of a separable verb; a reader does not need to settle that argument. The practical point is that the absence of er, daar, hier or waar does not prove that the short word belongs to the finite verb. Look left for a route, boundary or period as well as looking back to the verb.

The wrong parse

What a dictionary gives you

betaalt → to pay · terug → back

Both correct. Assembled into "pays back" they even look right — until the next letter, where afwijzen yields "points away" and the method quietly fails.

What to look up

terugbetalen

Join first, look up once. The joined form is a dictionary entry; the halves are a guess that happens to work sometimes.

Three to try

Find the finite verb, go to the end of the clause, join what you find, and only then read the middle. Write down the joined infinitive for each before you open the answers — the infinitive, not the form printed on the page. One of these three contains a short word at the front of a verb that must not be pulled off.

  1. Vul het formulier volledig in en stuur het vóór 1 juni naar ons terug.
  2. Wij wijzen uw bezwaar af omdat u de termijn heeft overschreden.
  3. Omdat wij uw gegevens nog niet volledig hebben ontvangen, stellen wij de behandeling van uw bezwaar uit.
Show the worked parses

One. Two instructions, two separable verbs, both split: vul … in is invullen, to fill in, and stuur … terug is terugsturen, to send back. Each particle sits at the end of its own clause, which is why the sentence has two of them. Instructions in Dutch letters are built almost entirely from this class of verb, so a paragraph telling you what to do will have a particle at the end of nearly every sentence.

Two. The main clause splits: wijzen … af is afwijzen, to reject. The subordinate clause does not split, and this is the contrast to notice — overschreden keeps its over- attached, because overschrijden (to exceed) is one of the inseparable ones. Same short word at the front, entirely different behaviour, in a single sentence. The reason it is overschreden rather than overgeschreden is chapter 04's.

Three. The subordinate clause comes first, so by chapter 02 the main clause opens with its verb: stellen. Its partner is the last word of the sentence, six words later — uit, giving uitstellen, to postpone. Everything between them is what has been postponed. Read the halves separately and you have a sentence about placing something outside, which is not what arrived in the post.

Did it work?

You have joined a stranded short word to a verb. Five tests, and the first two are the ones that catch a wrong join outright.

  • Join to the infinitive, not to what is printed. The page gives you vul; the dictionary wants invullen, not invul. If the form you are about to look up does not end in -en, you have not finished the join, and the lookup will fail for a reason that has nothing to do with your reading.
  • The joined form must be a dictionary entry. That is the whole claim of this chapter: the halves are a guess, the join is a lookup. If the joined infinitive is not in the dictionary, the short word was not a particle — go back and look for one of the three things that are not the particle.
  • Check for er, daar, hier, waar. If one of those four is already sitting in the clause, the stranded preposition may belong to it rather than to the verb (chapter 07). Their absence removes that candidate; it does not prove the join.
  • Look immediately to the left of the short word. A route, boundary or period can form a unit with it: de grens over, het gebouw uit, de hele dag door. Translate that whole phrase before joining anything to the verb. A dictionary entry for the joined verb is evidence that the join is possible, not proof that this sentence uses it.
  • The joined meaning should beat the separate one. Not merely fit — beat it. If reading the halves separately gives you an equally good sentence, you may be looking at the case in the next box, and you should not force a choice.

Where this procedure cannot decide

A direction phrase can produce exactly the shape the procedure is looking for:

Wij rijden morgen de grens over.

The safe reading is we cross the border tomorrow: read de grens over together. Joining over to rijden also produces a real dictionary verb, but that fact alone does not tell you whether the sentence contains that lexical verb or a directional phrase. Here the distinction does not change the practical meaning. In another sentence it may, so a successful lookup is not a verdict.

There is a second ambiguity, this time inside the verb itself.

The variable prefixes — voor-, over-, door-, om-, onder- — form two different verbs from the same letters, one separable and one not. When the verb splits, the split is your evidence. When it does not split, sometimes nothing is.

Dat kan voorkomen.

Two readings, both grammatical. Voorkomen with the stress on the particle is the separable verb, to occur: that can happen. With the stress on the stem it is the inseparable verb, to prevent: that can prevent [something]. Written down, the two are the same seven letters in the same position, and reading has no stress.

Three things narrow it, and none of them is the procedure. A te wedged inside — om dit voor te komen — proves the separable one, because only a separable verb takes te between its halves. A participle disambiguates too: voorgekomen is the separable verb, voorkomen the inseparable one, which is chapter 04's business. And to prevent needs an object, so if the clause has no candidate for one, the reading is occur.

Where none of those is present, the sentence is genuinely ambiguous on the page and you are entitled to say so. The same holds for ondergaan and a handful of others. Recording a sentence as ambiguous is a better outcome than picking the reading you happened to think of first — a Dutch reader hearing the sentence would have information you do not have.

Step 4 In your own post

Take the letter you used for chapter 02 — you already have its list of clause-final words. Go back through that list and mark every entry that is one of the particles above. Each mark is a verb you have been reading in halves, and joining it to the finite verb at the front of its clause will usually change what the sentence says.

Two places in a letter are almost pure separable verbs. Any paragraph telling you to do something — invullen, opsturen, terugsturen, doorgeven, indienen, bijvoegen, inloggen. And any sentence about money moving — overmaken, terugbetalen, uitbetalen, inhouden, aftrekken. Those are the two kinds of sentence in the letter you most need to get right, and both are made of the construction that is hardest to look up.

One habit is worth more than the rest: when a lookup produces a plausible but oddly vague sentence, suspect a split verb before you suspect your vocabulary. "We make the amount to your account" is not a sentence anyone wrote. It is what a correct lookup of two correct words produces, and the vagueness is the signal.

Run the same five tests from step 3 on every join you make. The infinitive test costs nothing and removes the commonest reason a correct reading fails at the dictionary; the dictionary test is the one that tells you the join was wrong at all.

Then mark what you could not settle, in three piles. First, any clause where a short word sat at the end and the joined form was not in the dictionary — that is not a particle, and the sentence is still open. Second, any short word immediately preceded by a route, boundary or period: test de grens over or het hele jaar door as one phrase before you join the short word to the verb. Third, and more important, any verb beginning voor-, over-, door-, om- or onder- that appears unsplit. If there is no te inside it and no participle to go on, you may be looking at either of two verbs, and the letter does not say which. Write both readings down. A sentence you have correctly identified as ambiguous is one you will not misact on; a sentence you resolved by guessing is one you might.

References

Donaldson treats separable and inseparable verbs together at printed pages 180–184, which is the right pairing: the two classes are only intelligible against each other, and the variable prefixes belong to neither until you see what a given sentence does with them.

Taaltalent deel 1 gives separable verbs two units, and deel 2 returns to the separable/inseparable distinction; De Opmaat's grammar reference has its own section for them under the verb. The facts are taught, early and twice. What the courses present is the formation — how to build the split form when you are producing a sentence. The reading direction is the other one, and it is the harder of the two: not "where do I put the particle", but "there is a stray word at the end of this clause and I need to know which verb it belongs to."

Kaart

Nieuw

  • Separable verbs: particle plus verb, one dictionary entry
  • Split only when finite in a main clause; joined elsewhere
  • te goes inside: over te maken
  • Six inseparable prefixes: be-, er-, ge-, her-, ont-, ver-
  • The meaning is often not the sum of the halves
  • For variable prefixes, the split itself tells you which verb you have
  • A postposition can mimic a stranded particle: de grens over, het huis uit
  • Test the join, then look it up once; a successful lookup is evidence, not proof

Terug

  • After you find the finite verb, where do you look next — and why is that not the end of the sentence? (ch. 02)

    The end of the clause. A subordinating conjunction such as omdat starts a new clause with a final verb of its own, and Dutch need not mark the boundary with a comma. This chapter says what to do with what you find there.

  • Is the stranded particle always the last word of the clause? (ch. 02)

    No. Material can be pushed out past it — … over op uw rekening. Read the word that closes the packed middle, not the one nearest the full stop.

  • A compound is read from its right-hand element. Why does that not work here? (ch. 01)

    Because a separable verb is not compositional. The structure is right-headed, but the meaning does not add up: overmaken is not over plus make.
