Part III · Who must do what — Chapter 09

Nobody does anything

Chapter 08 left you with a blank line. A letter had required something and had not said of whom. That is not carelessness — it is two specific constructions doing exactly what they are for, and both of them can be undone.

Before you start (ch. 08)

You meet dienen in a letter. What tells you whether it is an obligation or the ordinary verb meaning to serve?

Answer

Whether it governs an infinitive with te. A te plus infinitive somewhere in the verb group means obligation; a noun phrase or preposition instead — ter onderbouwing — means the lexical verb and no obligation at all.

The device Official Dutch has two habits that remove the actor from the front of a sentence. It makes the verb passive, so the thing acted on becomes the subject; and it turns the verb into a noun, so there is no subject slot at all. Neither construction deletes the actor. Both push it somewhere else in the sentence, or out of it and into your knowledge of who sent the letter.

Step 1 On the page

Three sentences from the middle of a decision letter.

Betreft: uw bezwaar

Uw aanvraag wordt binnen acht weken beoordeeld.

Het bezwaar is door de inspecteur afgewezen.

Na ontvangst van uw betaling wordt de aanslag aangepast.

Read them for who does what and the first and third give you nothing. Something will be assessed; something will be adjusted. By whom is not in either sentence, and a reader who wants to know whom to ring has nowhere to look.

The second is different, and the difference is worth seeing before the explanation. It has the same shape — a form of zijn or worden early, a participle at the end — but four words in the middle name a person. Door de inspecteur. The actor was there all along; it simply was not where a reader looks for it.

And the third has done something else again. Ontvangst is not a verb on the page, it is a noun, and it is standing in for the whole event of somebody receiving something. Who receives? The noun does not have room to say. The verb was turned into a thing, and the actor went with it.

These two habits — the passive and the noun-shaped verb — account for most of the sentences in official Dutch that leave you unsure whether you have understood or merely decoded.

Step 2 What it is doing

The passive: the thing acted on becomes the subject

A passive sentence promotes the object. De inspecteur wijst het bezwaar af has the inspector as subject; het bezwaar wordt afgewezen has the objection as subject and no room for the inspector unless the sentence chooses to make room.

The clearest way to see both habits is one proposition written three ways. Nothing changes about what happens; only the actor's visibility changes.

De gemeente beoordeelt uw aanvraag. Active. Everyone is named, and the actor is the first word.
Uw aanvraag wordt door de gemeente beoordeeld. Passive. The application is now the subject; the council has moved into a door phrase in the middle. Still named — just late.
Na beoordeling van uw aanvraag ontvangt u een besluit. Nominalised. The assessing has become a noun, and there is now no subject position for a council to occupy at all.

The auxiliary is worden, not zijn — this is where English intuition misleads, because English builds its passive with to be. And the agent, where it appears, is introduced by door.

TenseDutchEnglish
presenthet bezwaar wordt afgewezenis (being) rejected
imperfecthet bezwaar werd afgewezenwas (being) rejected
perfecthet bezwaar is afgewezenhas been rejected
pluperfecthet bezwaar was afgewezenhad been rejected
futurehet bezwaar zal afgewezen wordenwill be rejected

The middle two rows are the ones that catch readers, and there is a reason they look wrong. The perfect passive ought to be is afgewezen geworden — two participles — and Dutch will not tolerate that, so it drops the geworden. What is left, is afgewezen, looks exactly like a description of a state rather than a report of an event. Hold that thought; it is this chapter's unresolved case.

Nominalisation: the verb becomes a noun

The second habit is simpler and less often named. Take the verb, make it a noun, and the sentence no longer has a place where a subject would go.

wij ontvangen uw betaling → na ontvangst van uw betaling "We receive your payment" becomes "after receipt of your payment". The we has nowhere to be.
u dient het bezwaar in → het indienen van het bezwaar "You submit the objection" becomes "the submitting of the objection". Same disappearance, different mechanism: this one is the plain infinitive used as a noun.

Two shapes cover most of them, and you can recognise both on sight.

  • The infinitive used as a noun, always with het: het indienen, het invullen, het betalen. If you see het in front of something ending in -en, it is a verb wearing a noun's clothes.
  • Nouns in -ing, always de: de beoordeling, de berekening, de behandeling. Chapter 01 promised you these — they look like compounds and are not; they are verbs with a suffix, and chapter 05's rule that -ing takes de is the same fact seen from the other side. But the ending alone does not make a nominalisation. De woning, de afdeling and de rekening end the same way and name a dwelling, a department and a bill — things, not actions. The test is whether the noun names something happening, not how it is spelled.

There are also bare deverbal nouns with no suffix at all — ontvangst, aanvraag, verzoek, besluit. These are the hardest to spot, because nothing about them announces that a verb is inside. The signal is functional rather than formal: a preposition followed by a noun that names an event — na ontvangst, bij aanvraag, na behandeling — is almost always a clause that has been compressed.

Backgrounded, not deleted

This is the point the chapter turns on, and it is worth stating flatly. Neither construction removes the actor from existence. A passive can carry its agent in a door phrase, and a nominalisation can carry one too — de beoordeling door de inspecteur. The actor may be present and merely late; it may be recoverable from the letterhead; or it may genuinely not be stated. Those are three different findings and a reader who collapses them into "unclear" has thrown away most of the information.

Late, not absent

Het bezwaar is door de inspecteur afgewezen.

Actor named, four words after the auxiliary. Write down the inspector.

Genuinely unstated

Uw aanvraag wordt binnen acht weken beoordeeld.

No agent anywhere. The actor is almost certainly the body that sent the letter — but the sentence does not say so, and that is worth recording as an inference rather than a reading.

One more way Dutch avoids naming an actor, because you will meet it and it is not a passive at all: men. It is an active sentence with a subject that means one — men verzoekt u. Formal, and vanishingly rare in speech. Treat it as a filled subject slot with nobody in it.

A caution about how far to take all this. Dutch as a whole is not especially fond of the passive — it uses it less than English does, and often prefers an active with men or je. What is true is that the official register leans on it heavily. So a cluster of passives is not a fact about the Dutch language; it is a fact about the kind of document you are holding, which is chapter 11's subject.

Step 3 Read it

The procedure

  1. Bracket the clause and find the participle at the end (ch. 02, 04).
  2. Look at the auxiliary. A form of worden — wordt, werd, worden — is a passive outright. A form of zijn may be a passive perfect; see step 5.
  3. Search the middle field for door. If it is there and followed by a person or a body, you have the actor and the sentence is finished.
  4. If there is no door, look for a nominalisation — het + infinitive, a -ing noun, or a preposition plus an event noun.
  5. Unfold it back into a clause. This is the step that does the work. wordt beoordeeld → somebody assesses something. het indienen van bezwaar → somebody submits an objection. Say it as a sentence with a subject, even if the subject is the word somebody, and the shape of what is missing becomes visible.
  6. Fill the Actor line, and grade the evidence: stated, recoverable, probable, or not stated.

Applied to step 1's third sentence. Wordt … aangepast is a plain present passive, so the assessment is the thing acted on. There is no door. And na ontvangst van uw betaling is a preposition plus an event noun — a compressed clause meaning once we have received your payment. So the sentence contains two actions and names an actor for neither. The completed extraction, carrying chapter 08's format forward:

From the sentence
Statusnot required — this describes what the office will do
Actorprobable — the tax office, from the letterhead. Not stated in the sentence, and the grammar does not prove it
Actionthe assessment is adjusted, once your payment has arrived
Deadlinenone stated — and the trigger is your payment, not a date

That last line is the practical yield. The sentence looked like a deadline and is not one. Nothing happens on a date; something happens after you act. A reader who files this letter as "they will adjust it within eight weeks" has misread which of the two parties the clock is waiting for.

You can stop here and use the procedure. Five steps: bracket, check the auxiliary, hunt for door, look for a noun-shaped verb, and say which kind of missing actor you have. Everything below is the ways it misfires, the case it cannot settle, and how Part III closes.

Three to try

For each, decide whether it is passive, and fill the Actor line with one of the four evidence levels. Write the answers down before opening them. One of the three is not a passive at all, though it looks like one.

  1. De aanslag is op 3 juni door de inspecteur vastgesteld.
  2. Na beoordeling van de door u ingediende stukken wordt een besluit genomen.
  3. De termijn voor het indienen van een bezwaarschrift is verstreken.
Show the worked answers

One. Is … vastgesteld, a perfect passive with the geworden dropped, and door de inspecteur sitting in the middle field. Actor: named. Note the order — date, then agent, then the participle last, which is chapter 02's middle field packed exactly as it always is. Status: not required, this reports what has been done.

Two. Two constructions stacked. Na beoordeling van … is a nominalisation, and inside it sits chapter 06's left-hand block — de door u ingediende stukken, whose head is stukken, the documents submitted by you. So that phrase names your actorhood but not the assessor's. Then wordt een besluit genomen, a plain present passive with no door. Actor: genuinely probable for the deciding — the sender is the obvious candidate and the sentence does not say so. And notice een besluit nemen has itself become passive around a noun that is also a compressed verb.

Three. This one is not a passive. Verstrijken — to elapse — is intransitive: a deadline elapses, nobody elapses it. So is verstreken is an ordinary active perfect that happens to be built with zijn, exactly like ik ben gekomen. There is no hidden actor because there is no action done to anything. Actor: not applicable. The sentence does contain a nominalisation — het indienen van — but it is inside a noun phrase naming the deadline, not the main clause's verb. What the sentence actually tells you is that it is too late, which is the single most consequential sentence shape in this book so far.

Did it work?

You have called a sentence passive and filled in an actor. Five tests, and the first three can each show the reading is wrong.

  • A passive needs an auxiliary. A participle with no form of worden or zijn governing it is not a passive clause — most often it is chapter 06's participle standing in front of a noun, as in de ingediende stukken. Those do carry a hidden actor, but they are not the construction you are analysing.
  • Zijn plus a participle is not automatically passive. Verbs that take zijn in the active perfect — verstrijken, komen, gebeuren, blijken — produce the identical shape. The test is whether the verb can take an object at all. If nobody can do it to something, it is not a passive and there is no missing actor to find.
  • Door is only the agent when what follows can act. It is also an ordinary preposition meaning through or because of: door een fout in de berekening is a cause, not a person. A body or a person after it is an agent; a reason, a route or a period is not.
  • Inside a nominalisation, van marks the thing acted on, not the actor. De beoordeling van uw aanvraag is the assessment of your application — the application is what gets assessed, and recording it as the actor inverts the sentence. The agent of a nominalisation, when it appears, uses door: de beoordeling door de gemeente. Check for a door before concluding the actor is absent.
  • Grade the evidence, in four levels. Stated — a door phrase or an active subject. Recoverable — named elsewhere in the letter, or the sentence addresses u directly. Probable — the sender is the obvious candidate and nothing says so. Not stated — nothing at all. A letterhead is context, not grammatical proof, and the difference between recoverable and probable is exactly the difference between a reading and a guess.

Where this procedure cannot decide

Because Dutch drops the geworden from the perfect passive, is plus a participle has two readings that the form cannot separate.

De aanslag is vastgesteld.

Read as a perfect passive it reports an event: the assessment has been established, by somebody, presumably recently. Read as a state it is a description: the assessment is established — settled, final, the participle behaving like an adjective in the way the door is closed does in English. In that second reading no agent is implied at all.

For a reader of a tax letter those are different letters. One says a person has just done something and there may be a step you can take about it; the other says the matter is closed. The form is identical and Dutch expects context to decide.

Three things narrow it, and none is the sentence itself. A door phrase forces the event reading — a state has no agent. A date does the same: is op 3 juni vastgesteld is an event, because states do not happen on Tuesdays. And a wordt in the same paragraph tells you the letter is describing a process rather than a settled position. With none of the three present, record both readings. This is the one place in the book where the two readings differ in whether anything remains to be done, and guessing is the most expensive guess a reader of official post can make.

Step 4 In your own post

You already have the list. Take the four-line extractions from chapter 08 and go back to fill the Actor lines you left blank — that was the exercise's whole purpose, and this chapter is the tool for it. Add one column while you do it, because the Actor line is now worth only as much as the evidence behind it.

The third row is the one the column exists for. It looks like the second and it is not the same thing: one is a fact the letter states, the other is a guess the letter permits.

The search is two words long. Find every wordt, werd and worden, then every door. The first set finds the passives; the second finds the actors that are present but late. In a two-page decision letter that is usually a dozen hits and it will resolve most of the blanks.

Then run the second pass, for nominalisations, and it is a different kind of looking: prepositions followed by event nouns — na ontvangst, bij beoordeling, na afloop, voor het indienen. Each one is a clause that has been folded up, and unfolding it back into a sentence with a subject is the single most useful thing you can do to an official paragraph.

Where they cluster is worth knowing. The paragraph explaining a decision is almost pure passive, because its job is to report what was done without dwelling on who did it. And the paragraph setting conditions is where the nominalisations are, because a condition is an event referred to rather than performed.

Run the same five tests from step 3 on every hit, and the second one especially — zijn plus a participle is the commonest thing in these letters and a good share of them are not passive at all.

Then record what stayed open, and expect two kinds. Any is plus participle with no agent, no date and no wordt nearby: mark it event or state, not determined. And every Actor line you graded probable rather than stated or recoverable — because that inference is usually right and occasionally very wrong, and a letter forwarding somebody else's decision is exactly where it fails.

Part III ends here

Two chapters, one question: who is required to do what, and by when? Chapter 08 gave you the verbs that carry an obligation and the three-way status that keeps an option from being filed as a demand. This chapter gave you the two constructions that hide the actor, and the distinction between an actor who is missing and one who is merely late.

The output is four lines per obligation-shaped sentence — status, actor, action, deadline — and you can now fill all four from a letter you could not previously read. What Part III has not given you is the connective tissue: whether an obligation applies at all, what it is conditional on, and what happens if it is not met. Those words are chapter 10, and they are short, common, and almost never taught.

References

Donaldson's chapter on the passive is §11.13, printed pages 161–165. The tense table in step 2 follows his at printed 161, and the sentence this chapter is built on is his: in the passive the agent of the action may be left unmentioned if so desired, but it is always implied. §11.13.3 (printed 162) gives the structure — subject + worden + optional door + participle — and states plainly that Dutch uses worden where English uses to be.

The dropped geworden and the unresolved case are both §11.13.4, printed 162–163. He sets out the action-versus-state ambiguity in the same terms used above and concludes that both readings are the same Dutch sentence and the context tells us what is intended — which is a grammarian saying, in 1981, that the procedure runs out. The same section notes that a repletive er is very common in the passive especially when the agent is not determined, which is one of the three uses of er chapter 07 fenced off, now doing a job this chapter cares about.

Two further sections connect this chapter backwards. At printed 164 he records that written style commonly replaces a relative clause containing a passive with an adjectival adjunct placed before the noun, cross-referencing the page chapter 06 was built on — so de ingediende stukken and de stukken die zijn ingediend are one construction, and chapters 06 and 09 have been describing it from two sides. At printed 165 he notes that the passive is more common in English than in Dutch, with men and je often used instead — the basis for the caution in step 2 against reading a pile of passives as a fact about the language rather than about the genre.

The gender facts behind the nominalisation section are §7.1, the same pages chapter 05 used: all infinitives used as nouns are neuter, printed 30, which is why it is always het indienen; and nouns in -ing are common gender, printed 28, which is why it is always de beoordeling. Neither course reaches the passive as a reading problem, and neither treats nominalisation at all — reasonably, since a learner building toward speech has no need to produce na ontvangst van uw betaling. A reader meets it in the first paragraph that sets a condition.

Kaart

Nieuw

  • One proposition, three shapes: active, passive, nominalised — the actor is named, late, or gone
  • The passive promotes the thing acted on to subject; the auxiliary is worden, not zijn
  • door introduces the agent — when what follows can act
  • The perfect passive drops geworden, which is why is afgewezen looks like a state
  • zijn + participle is not automatically passive — is verstreken is an active perfect
  • Nominalisation: het + infinitive (always neuter), -ing nouns (always de), and bare event nouns after a preposition
  • Inside a nominalisation, van marks the thing acted on; door marks the actor
  • An -ing ending is not enough — woning, afdeling and rekening name things, not actions
  • Backgrounded, not deleted — grade the actor stated / recoverable / probable / not stated, and never fill the line more confidently than the evidence allows
  • men — an active subject slot with nobody in it
  • A pile of passives is a fact about the genre, not about Dutch

Terug

  • What are the four lines you extract from an obligation-shaped sentence, and which one did chapter 08 leave blank? (ch. 08)

    Status, actor, action, deadline. The Actor line was the blank one, and filling it is what this chapter is for.

  • How do you get from a participle back to the dictionary verb? (ch. 04)

    Three passes: what job the form is doing, where the ge- is, and whether the ending reverses. Every passive in this chapter ends in a participle you recover that way.

  • A participle sits in front of a noun with no auxiliary anywhere. What is it — and does it still have an actor? (ch. 06)

    A left-hand modifier block, not a passive clause. It does still background an actor, which is why de door u ingediende stukken and de stukken die door u zijn ingediend say the same thing.

  • An official sentence contains a word ending in -ing. What does that tell you, and what does it not? (ch. 01, 05)

    That it takes de — chapter 05's rule. It does not tell you the word names an action: woning and afdeling are ordinary things. Chapter 01 flagged the class as looking like compounds without being them.
