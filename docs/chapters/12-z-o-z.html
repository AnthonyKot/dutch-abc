Part V · The real documents — Chapter 12

Z.o.z.

Eleven chapters have been about language — the words, their arrangement, and what the choice of them conveys. This one is about the marks on a document that are not words at all, and about where a fluent, confident read gives you the wrong number.

Before you start (ch. 11)

A message uses your first name, je and a diminutive. What has that told you, and what has it not?

Answer

It is evidence about the situation and the role the sender is performing — probably commercial or neighbourly rather than official. It is not evidence about the sender as a person, and it is no evidence at all about urgency or about whether anything is required of you. Those come from the obligation verb and the stated consequence.

The device A Dutch document writes its amounts, dates, times and stock phrases in a notation. Notation is not vocabulary, and that is what makes it dangerous: you cannot fail to read a number, so nothing stops you and nothing tells you. On three of the four groups in this chapter, an English-reading habit supplies a confident answer that is wrong — and on one of them it is wrong by a factor of a thousand.
Scope. This is not a vocabulary list to memorise. It is the small set of places where the notation and the habit disagree, plus one thing no earlier chapter could offer: a check the document runs on itself.

Step 1 On the page

Two fragments from the same utility. Nothing in either is grammatically hard, and by now you can parse every sentence in the second one.

Jaarafrekening · bedragen

Termijnbedrag per maand — € 125,-

In rekening gebracht, 12 termijnen — € 1.500,-

Werkelijk verbruik — € 1.732,45

Nog te betalen — € 232,45

Rente bij te late betaling — 2,750% per jaar

Jaarafrekening · bericht

Uw meteropname is gepland op 12-03-2026, tussen half twee en half vier.

Bent u dan niet thuis, geef dit dan uiterlijk a.s. vrijdag aan ons door.

De meterstanden t/m 31-12-2025 staan op de afrekening van jl. oktober.

Z.o.z. voor de specificatie per kwartaal.

There is no sentence here you cannot take apart. The second fragment has an inversion after a fronted condition, a separable verb with its particle at the end (geef … door), and a t/m phrase modifying a plural noun. Chapters 02, 03 and 06 handle all of it.

And a reader who has done eleven chapters can still get four things wrong here, in ways that cost money or a wasted afternoon. An English-trained eye reads € 1.500,- as one and a half euros. It reads 2,750% as two thousand seven hundred and fifty per cent — or, correcting for that, as something plausible but still not right. It reads half twee as half past two, which puts you out of the house when the meter reader arrives. And it has no idea whether 12-03-2026 is March or December.

None of these is a comprehension failure. Every one of them is a decoding failure that feels like comprehension, which is why eleven chapters of grammar do not touch it. The rest of this chapter is the notation, and then the habit that catches the errors anyway.

Step 2 What it is doing

The two marks

Dutch uses the full stop and the comma inside numbers the opposite way round from English. The full stop groups thousands; the comma marks the decimal.

On the pageValueThe habit says
10.000ten thousandten
€ 1.500,-fifteen hundred euros exactlyone euro fifty
€ 1.732,45one thousand seven hundred and thirty-two euros forty-five—
2,750%two and three-quarters per centtwo thousand seven hundred and fifty per cent

The trailing ,- is worth naming on its own: it is not a dash or a missing figure. It says and no cents, and it is extremely common on invoices and price lists.

Two further traps in the same family. Miljard is a thousand million — the number an English speaker calls a billion — so a miljard is not a miljoen scaled by anything you would guess. And an ordinal is written with a bare e where English writes st, nd or th: 1e, 2e, 122e. On a tax calculation, 1e schijf is the first band, not a quantity of anything.

Dates, and the words that move them

All-digit dates come in two shapes, and the four-digit group tells you which one you are looking at. If it is last, the date is day‑month‑year — 31-12-2025 is the last day of December, and this is the ordinary shape in Dutch letters. If the four-digit group is first, the date is year‑month‑day, which turns up in exports, filenames and software output. Check that before anything else, because the two shapes put the day at opposite ends.

Written out, the month is a lower-case word: 16 april 2026. Abbreviated, the list holds one surprise, which is that March is mrt.:

jan. · feb. · mrt. · apr. · mei · juni · juli · aug. · sept. · okt. · nov. · dec.

Three short words do more damage than the format does, because each of them silently moves a date:

  • t/m — tot en met, up to and including. A period given as t/m 31 december contains 31 December. Plain tot does not carry that guarantee, and where the difference matters a careful document uses t/m precisely because it is unambiguous.
  • a.s. — aanstaande, next. A.s. vrijdag is the Friday coming.
  • jl. — jongstleden, last. Jl. oktober is the October just gone.

A.s. and jl. differ by one letter and point in opposite directions in time. They are the reason this chapter is not filler.

The clock, and a word that does two jobs

Half before a clock hour counts backwards from it. Half twee is half past one; half drie is half past two. The Dutch name the hour they are heading towards, and English names the one just left.

DutchClock
kwart over een1:15
half twee1:30
tien over half twee1:40
kwart voor twee1:45

The third line is the one to sit with. Tien over half twee is built on half twee, not on twee — ten minutes past 1:30. Dutch measures the half hour from the half hour.

Then the same word, before a month, does something else entirely. Half maart is the middle of March, not thirty minutes before it and not the end of February. It sits in a small series that is worth having whole:

begin april · half maart · midden maart · medio maart · eind mei the beginning of April · the middle of March, three ways of writing it · the end of May

And one confusable pair that lives in exactly the documents this book is about. Kwartier is a quarter of an hour; kwartaal is a quarter of a year, three months. A bill charged per kwartaal arrives four times a year.

Abbreviations, and why they are a reading problem rather than a list

Administrative Dutch is dense with abbreviations, and they come in three kinds: those read aloud as the words they stand for, those read as letters (KLM), and those read as a word (NAVO). For a reader the distinction hardly matters. What matters is that an abbreviation is a phrase written short, so the phrase is what you need — and once you have it, chapter 01 applies to it unchanged. That is why an abbreviation generally takes the gender of its final noun: het KNMI, because instituut is neuter.

A small working set covers most of the ones that change what a sentence requires. The fuller list is at the end of the chapter, where a list belongs.

O.a. deserves the bold. It appears constantly in letters listing what you must supply, and it means the list you are reading is a sample of the requirement, not the requirement. A reader who takes an o.a. list as exhaustive has satisfied part of a condition and believes they have satisfied all of it.

Step 3 Read it

The procedure

The procedure is short, because the decoding is easy once you know it. The value is in the last two steps, which are the part no earlier chapter could give you.

  1. Mark every token that is not a word. Digits, anything with a full stop between letters, anything with a slash, anything with a currency sign. On a page of officialese there will be more than you expect.
  2. Decode each one with the two marks, the calendar and the clock.
  3. Fix the date shape before you read a single date, using three things in this order. Where does the four-digit year sit — last gives day‑month‑year, first gives year‑month‑day. Where did the document come from — a Dutch-language letter from a Dutch organisation writes day‑month‑year, and that is evidence you are entitled to use. And then, where the page offers it, a date whose opening pair is above twelve: that pair can only be a day, so it confirms the shape for the others written the same way. The confirmation matters most when the origin is not obvious — a booking, an export, an invoice from abroad — which is exactly when the question is live. A page can also mix a hand-typed date with one a system generated, so check any date that looks odd rather than assuming the whole sheet agrees.
  4. Cross-check the decode against the document. Do the amounts reconcile? Does the deadline fall after the letter's date? Does the period run from one stated date to another?
  5. If the cross-check fails, suspect your decode before you suspect the document. Then record whatever you still could not settle.

Step 4 is the point of the chapter. Numbers and dates can be checked against each other without knowing any Dutch — arithmetic and the calendar do not care what language they are written in. Every other chapter in this book gives you a reading and leaves you to judge it; this one gives you a reading the page itself can confirm or refuse.

What settles a reading, and in what order

Three kinds of evidence run through this chapter, and they are not interchangeable. Keeping them ranked is what stops the procedure from being either too timid or too confident.

  1. Explicit notation wins outright. T/m includes the named day. Nothing overrides a form that exists precisely to remove the doubt.
  2. Where the document came from selects between conventions. A Dutch-language letter from a Dutch sender normally establishes day‑month‑year — 05-08-2026 is 5 August — unless the document itself signals another convention, which a four-digit group in first position does. This is evidence and you are entitled to use it; it is simply not the last word.
  3. But provenance cannot remove variation that lives inside Dutch itself. Knowing a document is Dutch tells you which convention is in play. It does not help where the convention itself varies — and bare tot is exactly that case.

Where the document came from is evidence about which convention is in use. It is not permission to manufacture certainty where that convention itself varies. The two boxes after the exercises are levels two and three of this list, in that order, and the difference between them is the whole reason both exist.

Worked on step 1

The amounts first. Three tokens, and the fourth line is a subtraction:

125,- × 12 = 1.500,- The monthly instalment times twelve is exactly what was charged. The document has just confirmed both decodes at once.
1.732,45 − 1.500,- = 232,45 Actual consumption minus what was already charged is the amount still to pay — the fourth line, unchanged.

Now read the same three amounts with English habits: €1.25 a month, €1.50 charged, €1.73 consumed, €232.45 outstanding. The first two do not multiply, the middle two do not subtract, and the fourth is a hundred times the third. Nothing about the arithmetic is close. The wrong decode does not merely give a wrong number; it destroys a relationship that was sitting in the document waiting to be used.

The dates second. 31-12-2025 has 31 in the first position, so this document is day-first, and 12-03-2026 is therefore 12 March. The window tussen half twee en half vier is 13:30 to 15:30 — the English reading would have you home from 14:30 and out for the first hour of it. A.s. vrijdag is the coming Friday; jl. oktober is the October behind you; t/m 31-12-2025 includes 31 December; and per kwartaal is every three months. The z.o.z. says the specification you would need to check any of this is printed on the other side of the sheet.

You can stop here and use the procedure. Mark the non-words, decode them, fix the date shape from the year position and the document's origin, cross-check against the arithmetic and the calendar, and trust the cross-check over your decode. Everything below is practice, the case this cannot settle, and the reference list.

Three to try

Decode every non-word token, and say what each one does to the requirement. Write your answers down first. One of the three contains a contradiction that the cross-check is supposed to catch.

  1. Het bedrag van € 2.480,- dient uiterlijk 01-05-2026 te zijn bijgeschreven. Genoemd bedrag is excl. 21% btw.
  2. Stuur o.a. een kopie van uw legitimatie mee. De stukken moeten t/m 15-06-2026 binnen zijn.
  3. Deze aanslag betreft het 3e kwartaal. Datum aanslag: 04-07-2026. Betaling dient vóór 02-07-2026 te geschieden.
Show the worked answers

One. € 2.480,- is two thousand four hundred and eighty euros exactly. 01-05-2026 has no part above twelve, so the digits alone do not fix the shape — and they do not need to. The four-digit year is last, and dient … te zijn bijgeschreven with excl. is Dutch administrative writing from a Dutch sender. That settles it as day-first: 1 May. Hunting the page for a date above twelve here would be ceremony, not rigour. The consequential word is excl.: the tax is not in the figure, so the amount actually leaving your account is 21% more. Chapter 08 gives you dient … te zijn bijgeschreven — a requirement, passive, no actor named — and chapter 09 tells you the missing actor is you.

Two. O.a. is the whole answer. The copy of your identification is one of the documents required and the letter has not told you the others, so a reader who sends only that has met part of the condition. T/m 15-06-2026 includes 15 June, and 15 is above twelve, so this document is day-first and the deadline is 15 June.

Three. This is the one with the contradiction. The assessment is dated 04-07-2026 and payment is required vóór 02-07-2026 — two days before the document exists. Neither date has a part above twelve, so the digits alone do not settle the shape — but this is a Dutch assessment written in Dutch, which does, and it gives day-first. Read month-first instead and the letter is dated 7 April with a deadline of 7 February, which is worse rather than better.

But the fragment does contain a resolver, and it is not a number. Het derde kwartaal is July to September — the third quarter of the year, three months, nothing to do with a quarter of an hour. An assessment for that quarter, dated four days into it, is ordinary; one dated in April, three months before the quarter starts, is not. So day-first is much the better reading, and the deadline really does precede the letter.

Which leaves the cross-check having found something real and not repaired it. That is the correct outcome and not a failure of the method: the honest record is dated 4 July, deadline 2 July, and those cannot both be right — a sentence that tells you exactly what to query. Notice also where the deciding evidence came from. The arithmetic settled the amounts and the calendar settled nothing; what settled this was a fact about how a tax quarter works, read out of one abbreviation.

Did it work?

The first two can prove you wrong outright, which is unusual in this book and is the whole reason for the chapter.

  • If the amounts do not reconcile, you may not use any of them yet. Instalments times months, or total minus paid, should give the figure the document already prints. When it does not, work in this order: suspect your decode first, because it is wrong far more often than a supplier's arithmetic — then suspect a line you did not see — and only then the document. Exercise three is the case where the third answer was the right one, and it is rare.
  • If your deadline falls before the letter's date, one of the two is decoded wrongly — or the document says something it cannot mean, which you should record rather than quietly repair.
  • A comma inside a number is a decimal point. If you wrote down a percentage in the thousands, or an amount in the small change, read it again.
  • Half before a clock hour goes backwards; before a month it goes to the middle. If you converted half maart with the clock rule, you are a fortnight out.
  • Did you treat an o.a. list as complete? It never is. Neither is a requirement introduced by onder meer.
  • Did you establish the date shape before reading any date, and did you use where the document came from? A Dutch-language letter from a Dutch organisation is day-month-year and you may rely on that. Save the hunt for a date above twelve for the documents whose origin you cannot see — that is where it earns its keep.

A fork you can usually resolve

A single amount printed 1.500, in a document you are not sure was produced in the Netherlands. Dutch convention makes it fifteen hundred; an invoice generated by software configured elsewhere may mean one and a half. The two readings are three orders of magnitude apart, so guessing is not an option.

It resolves from the page in most cases, and cheaply. Two tests, in this order. Find a number containing both marks — 1.732,45 — because the comma there can only be the decimal, which makes the full stop the grouping mark. Or find one with two full stops — 1.234.567 — which no decimal notation produces. Failing both, run the arithmetic: in practice only one of the readings makes the totals work.

What does not work is counting the digits after the full stop, and this chapter's own material shows why. Three digits after the mark looks like grouping, but 2,750% from step 1 has exactly three digits after its mark and is a decimal — a rate quoted to three places. Written in English convention the same rate is 2.750, which the digit count would confidently misread as two thousand seven hundred and fifty.

This is a fork rather than an undecidable case because the document nearly always contains the evidence. It is in this chapter to make the habit explicit — settle the convention once, from the page, before decoding anything.

Where this procedure cannot decide

Bevestiging · voorwaarden

Wijzigingen kunt u doorgeven t/m 25 mei 2026.

Uw abonnement loopt tot 1 juni 2026.

The first line is settled and the second is not. T/m is tot en met, so 25 May is inside the window — the notation has a form for saying inclusive and this writer used it. The second line uses bare tot, and the page has not said whether 1 June is the last day of the subscription or the first day after it.

This is not a coin toss, and saying so matters. In Netherlands Dutch, tot 1 juni will usually be read as ending when 1 June begins — the named day excluded. That is the commoner reading and you should write it down as your working one. But bare tot is used both inclusively and exclusively, which is exactly why tot en met is the recommended form when the last day must be counted in. The wording alone cannot establish which you are holding.

So this is chapter 09's evidence grading arriving in the notation. The end date is probable, not stated — and the distinction is the same one you have been making about actors since Part III. Level three of the list above is where a reading stops being a fact about the page and becomes a fact about the odds.

Nothing in this chapter closes the gap. Arithmetic does not apply, the calendar does not apply, and the origin of the document — which fixed the date shape two boxes ago — tells you only that the Dutch convention is in play, and the Dutch convention is the thing that varies. This is a gap in what the document stated, not a gap in your Dutch, and reading the sentence more carefully will not close it.

It is tempting to argue from the line above: the writer used t/m when they meant inclusive, so bare tot one line later must be deliberate. That inference is not safe. A deliberate contrast and an inconsistent habit produce exactly the same two lines, and you cannot see which one you are holding.

So the honest record has two lines rather than one: the subscription runs to 1 June, probably not including it; the document does not state whether it does. One day, which on a cancellation deadline is the entire question. What you have actually learned is not the date — it is that the document had the unambiguous form available and did not use it, which is worth a telephone call rather than a guess.

Step 4 In your own post

This week wants a document with money in it. A jaaropgave, an annual utility statement, an insurance renewal, a bank letter — anything with more than one figure on it, because the whole method depends on figures that can be checked against each other.

Mark every non-word token before reading a single sentence. This is the opposite of the habit the book has built so far, and deliberately so: in the earlier chapters you found the verb first. Here you find the numbers first, because they are the part that will still be wrong after you have understood every word on the page.

Then do the arithmetic. Add the instalments. Subtract the total already paid. Check the percentage against the amount it is supposed to have produced. This takes two minutes and it is the only place in this book where the document will tell you whether you got it right.

Run the same tests from step 3 — and the first one on every document without exception. If the parts do not sum, do not conclude that the supplier has made a mistake; conclude that you have, and find which token.

Then record what stayed open, and there will be two kinds. Any period or deadline written with bare tot where it matters whether the named day is inside or outside — the document had t/m available and did not use it, so the correct entry is that the boundary is not stated. And any abbreviation you could not expand: write it down rather than reading past it, because the working set in step 2 was chosen for the ones that change what a sentence requires, and an unexpanded one may be another.

Chapter 13 is about where in a letter each of these things sits — including, as it turns out, where a t.a.v. line sits, which is what decides which of its two meanings is active. References

The numerals are Donaldson's chapter 14, printed 222–234. The two marks are pinned there twice over: printed 223 states that Dutch writes thousands with a full stop and not a comma, with 10.000 as the example, and printed 226 gives 0,5% as nul komma vijf procent. Printed 229 adds that prices are written with commas rather than decimal points.

That last one carries a caution worth stating. Its example is in guilders, because the book is from 1981 and the euro did not exist. The convention outlived the currency — the specimen letters behind this book use it in euros throughout — but the illustration is obsolete, and that is the shape of this source generally: reliable about structure, dated in its particulars.

The ordinal written with a bare e is printed 226, where Donaldson notes that English st, nd and th are all rendered this way. Miljard as one thousand million is in the cardinal table, printed 222.

The clock is printed 230, where half twee is glossed as half past one and the over / voor half constructions are set out; the same page records that kwartier on its own means a quarter of an hour, and printed 226 that kwartaal is a quarter of a year. Dates are printed 231 — the month abbreviations, the modern lower case, and begin / half / medio / eind with a month name. The two senses of half come from two different pages of the same chapter, which is presumably why nothing puts them side by side.

The abbreviations are Appendix 3, printed 260–264, including the three kinds, the rule that an abbreviation takes the gender of its final noun, and z.o.z. itself at printed 264.

Two of the commonest abbreviations in present-day administrative Dutch — i.v.m. for in verband met and m.b.t. for met betrekking tot — are not in that appendix, though neighbours such as i.p.v. are. They are given here from observation and marked accordingly. The absence is itself mildly informative, in the same way that chapter 10 found echter missing from the conjunction lists: a reference list of common abbreviations is a snapshot of which phrases were common, and administrative writing has moved since.

On what the courses do: both De Opmaat and Taaltalent teach the numbers and the clock, and Taaltalent 2 teaches the ordinals — none of this is withheld material. What no course on this shelf shows is the notation as a document prints it: the grouping full stop, a decimal comma inside a percentage, 1e schijf on a calculation, t/m against bare tot, and the abbreviation density of a page of officialese. That is a claim about where the material appears, not about whether anyone teaches it.

Terug

  • An abbreviation stands for a phrase of several nouns. Which one gives it its de or het? (ch. 01, 05)

    The last one — het KNMI because instituut is neuter. It is chapter 01's rightmost-element rule doing the same job it does inside a compound, and chapter 05's reason for caring about gender at all.

  • A deadline sentence reads dient uiterlijk 01-05-2026 te zijn bijgeschreven. Who has to do something? (ch. 08, 09)

    Nobody is named. Dient te makes it a requirement and the passive removes the actor, so the actor is recoverable rather than stated — and in a letter addressed to you it is you. The Actor line goes down as recoverable, not as stated.

  • Why is a fragment like Bent u dan niet thuis, geef dit dan door readable even though it opens with a verb? (ch. 02, 03)

    A fronted condition inverts the main clause, and geef … door is a separable verb whose particle waits at the end. Both were the point of Part I, and the sentence is only hard if you look up geven and door separately.

  • A letter states a deadline in a friendly tone and no consequence. How urgent is it? (ch. 10, 11)

    The tone tells you nothing about it. Urgency comes from the stated consequence, and if the letter states none, that is what you record. A date is a fact about the letter; what happens after it is a separate claim the letter may simply not make.
