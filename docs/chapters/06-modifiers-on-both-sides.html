Part II · What refers to what — Chapter 06

Modifiers on both sides

A clause that describes a noun normally comes after it. Formal Dutch can pick the whole clause up and set it down in front — inside the noun phrase, between the article and the noun. It looks like a different construction. It is the same one, reversed. This is the heaviest chapter so far: it spends chapters 02, 04 and 05 at once. Stay with the procedure; the core route is short even when the examples are not.

Before you start (ch. 05)

A die follows two nouns, both plural. What has the pointer told you?

Answer

Nothing. All plurals take de and therefore die, so the spelling was forced and eliminates neither candidate. This chapter stacks these clauses up, which makes that limit sharper rather than softer.

The device A noun can be modified from the right by a relative clause, and from the left by a block wedged between its article and itself. Both say the same kind of thing about the noun, both contain a hole where the noun belongs, and formal Dutch uses whichever suits it. Recognising that they are the same device is most of what makes the left-hand version readable.

Step 1 On the page

Two sentences from the dense part of a letter. The first modifies one noun from both directions at once.

In the style of an official letter

Het door u ingevulde formulier dat wij op 3 juni ontvingen, bevat een fout.

De inspecteur die uw bezwaar behandelt, heeft de gegevens die u ons stuurde nog niet ontvangen.

The second sentence is long but nothing in it is strange: two relative clauses, each sitting behind its own noun, each sending its verb to the end of its own stretch of text exactly as chapter 02 said it would. You can read it slowly and get there.

The first sentence is where reading stops, and it stops at the second word. After het you expect a noun. What arrives is door, which is a preposition, so the natural move is to start reading a prepositional phrase — and then u arrives, and the phrase ends, and nothing has been said yet. Four words in, an experienced reader of English has no structure to hang anything on.

The sentence is not difficult. Het … formulier is a perfectly ordinary noun phrase, and door u ingevulde has been inserted into the middle of it. Once you know that is a thing Dutch does, the sentence takes about as long to read as the second one.

Step 2 What it is doing

First, the ordinary side

Chapter 05 gave you the machinery already: a relative clause follows its noun, opens with a pointer chosen to match that noun's gender and number, and contains a gap where the noun belongs. Three additions are all this chapter needs on that side.

They stack, and each keeps its own verb at the end. The second sentence in step 1 has two of them, and the only thing that makes it long is that each relative clause obeys chapter 02 independently. Find the end of each clause and you have found each verb.

When the pointer needs a preposition, the form changes. For a person, the preposition simply comes first with wie:

de persoon aan wie u de sleutel geeft

For a thing, Dutch does not say aan die. It welds the preposition onto waar-:

de brief waarin wij de termijn noemen

That is waar plus in, and it means in which. There is a whole family of these — waarvan, waarbij, waarop — and they can come apart and sit at opposite ends of a clause. All of that is chapter 07. Here you need only to recognise a waar- word as a pointer with a preposition attached, and to know that the preposition is doing ordinary prepositional work.

And formal writing has a relative pronoun of its own. Where a course teaches die and dat, older and more legalistic Dutch sometimes writes welke:

de documenten welke u moet meesturen

Donaldson records welk(e) as often replacing die in formal writing. Treat that as a description of 1981, not of now. It reads as dated today, plain die is generally preferred, and a letter that uses it is telling you something about the institution that sent it. It carries no extra meaning, and the only thing a reader has to do with it is not be stopped by it. There is also wat, the usual pointer after alles, iets and niets — dat occurs there too — and the required one after a whole preceding clause:

alles wat u voor 1 juni aan ons stuurt

Now the mirror

Here is the move the chapter exists for. A relative clause built around a completed action can very often be compressed and packed in front of the noun instead, between it and its article. Donaldson treats these as adjectival adjuncts that replace relative clauses, and the honest way to learn them is in pairs, because the two members say the same thing:

Modifier on the rightThe same thing on the left
het formulier dat u heeft ingevuld het door u ingevulde formulier
de brief die wij u in maart stuurden de in maart aan u gestuurde brief
de beslissing die de inspecteur op 3 juni heeft genomen de op 3 juni door de inspecteur genomen beslissing

Read the right-hand column on its own and it looks like a foreign construction. Read it across from the left-hand column and it is obviously the same sentence with the furniture moved. The article and the noun still belong together — het … formulier, de … brief, de … beslissing — and everything between them is the modifier.

The gap test from chapter 05 still works, and it is the reason to trust the pairing. In het door u ingevulde formulier the block is door u ___ ingevulde: filled in by you, but filled in what? There is a hole, and the noun on the right of the block fills it. That is precisely what a relative clause does; the only difference is which side of the noun the hole sits on.

One word in these blocks deserves naming now, because it recurs. Door marks who performed the action — door u, door de inspecteur. That is the same signal chapter 09 is built on, and you will meet it constantly before then. Here you only need to read it, not analyse it.

What English can and cannot do

It is tempting to say English cannot do this at all. That is not true and the true version is more useful. English puts participles in front of nouns constantly — the completed form, the enclosed documents, a signed declaration. The structure is familiar.

What English refuses

the by-you-filled-in form

Not a sentence anyone would write. English will not carry an agent phrase in front of the noun.

What English does instead

the form filled in by you

The moment there is a by, English sends the whole thing to the right of the noun. Dutch does not have to.

So the difference is narrow and specific: Dutch can put a long modifier, including its agent and its time and place, in front of the noun, and English can only put a short bare participle there. That single fact accounts for nearly all the difficulty, and it is why the blocks that stop you are always the ones with several words in them.

Two spelling details you need

The participle in one of these blocks inflects exactly as an adjective would, which after de or het means it takes an -e: ingevuld becomes ingevulde, gestuurd becomes gestuurde. Chapter 04's three passes still recover the verb; the -e comes off first and changes nothing else.

Because it is ordinary adjective inflection, it follows the ordinary exception too. After een, a neuter singular takes no ending:

het door u ingevulde formulier · een door u ingevuld formulier Same construction, same participle, and the -e appears only in the definite one. Do not treat a missing -e as evidence that this is not a left-hand block.

Participles already ending in -en — the strong ones — do not add anything: de op 3 juni genomen beslissing, de ontvangen brief. If a block ends in -en rather than -e, that is chapter 04's signal that you are looking at a strong participle, not evidence that something is missing.

How long these get

Donaldson notes that these adjuncts can reach unwieldy lengths, and quotes a newspaper sentence in which the block runs to more than a dozen words before the noun finally arrives. Official correspondence is milder than journalism, but the principle is the same: there is no grammatical limit, so the block ends when the writer decides it ends. This is the one construction in this book where the honest advice is that the reading procedure matters more than any amount of vocabulary.

Step 3 Read it

The procedure

Four moves, and the first is the one that tells you where to investigate.

  1. When an article is followed by material an ordinary adjective cannot begin, suspect a block. Het followed by door, de followed by in or a date — those are strong signals. Merely finding something other than a noun is not: de nieuwe brief is an ordinary noun phrase, with one adjective between the article and its noun.
  2. Find the participle that closes the block. There is always one — Donaldson notes that these adjuncts always contain a participle — and it is the last word belonging to the block. It will end in -e or -en: ingevulde, verstuurde, genomen.
  3. Continue to the first noun after that participle. That noun is the head governed by the opening article. Another ordinary adjective may still intervene: het door u ingevulde nieuwe formulier. The word after ingevulde is nieuwe; the first noun is formulier.
  4. Read the block as a relative clause, with the head filling its gap.

Do not simply take the first noun you meet. That shortcut looks equivalent and is not, because a left-hand block can contain noun phrases of its own — usually with their own article, which is the giveaway. In de door de gemeente verstuurde brief the first noun is gemeente, which belongs to the modifier and not to the de you started from. The head is brief, three words further on, on the far side of the participle. A second article inside the block means you have not reached the head yet. Once you pass the participle, however, a further adjective can still stand before the real head. The safe shortcut is the first noun after the participle, not simply the next word.

Reading forwards

Het door u …

Door is a preposition, so a phrase must be starting. It ends at u and has said nothing. Four words in, with no structure — and the temptation is to conclude the sentence is beyond you.

Finding the participle first

Het [door u ingevulde] formulier

Ingevulde closes the block, so the first noun after it is the head. Here it also happens to be the next word. The form. Which form? The one you filled in. Two pieces, in the order that makes them intelligible rather than the order they were printed in.

Now the whole of step 1's first sentence. The head is formulier, modified on the left by door u ingevulde and on the right by dat wij op 3 juni ontvingen. Both blocks describe the same noun, both have a gap in them that the noun fills, and the sentence's actual claim — bevat een fout — is four words long and arrives last. The form you filled in, which we received on 3 June, contains an error.

Three to try

For each sentence, name the head noun of every modified phrase before you open the answers. Write the heads down. The first sentence is built so that the fastest plausible method gets it wrong, and you will only find out whether you used that method if your answer is on paper before the worked version is on screen.

  1. De door de gemeente verstuurde nieuwe brief bevat een termijn die u niet mag overschrijden.
  2. Alles wat u voor 1 juni aan ons stuurt, nemen wij mee in de beoordeling.
  3. De persoon aan wie u de sleutel geeft, moet het formulier ondertekenen dat wij hebben meegestuurd.
Show the worked answers

One. One noun modified from each side, but they are different nouns. De is followed by door, so move one fires. This sentence punishes two shortcuts: the first noun after the article is gemeente, and the first word after the participle is nieuwe. Neither is the head. Find the participle — verstuurde — then continue to the first noun after it: brief. The block is door de gemeente verstuurde — sent by the council — and nieuwe is an ordinary second modifier on the same noun. Then the sentence proper begins, and een termijn picks up its own modifier on the right, die u niet mag overschrijden, with die correct because termijn is a de-word. Left block, right clause, one sentence.

Two. Wat is the usual pointer after alles, and it is what you will mostly see — but dat also occurs here in current standard Dutch, so this is a preference rather than a rule. What is not optional is wat after a whole preceding clause. Either way the gap test settles the question that matters, which is pointer or conjunction: u voor 1 juni aan ons ___ stuurt is missing its object. Note also that the whole modified phrase is sitting in first position, which is why the main clause opens with its verb — chapter 02's inversion, doing exactly what it always does.

Three. Aan wie, because the head is a person. Then the main clause, and its relative clause has been pushed out past the verb: het formulier ondertekenen dat wij hebben meegestuurd. That is chapter 02's tail again, and it is common precisely here — a heavy relative clause is exactly the sort of thing Dutch prefers to move to the end rather than leave in the middle. The pointer is dat, so it reaches back past ondertekenen to het formulier, the only neuter singular available.

Did it work?

You have opened an article, walked past a block, and named a head noun. Five tests. The first three can each tell you outright that the head is wrong.

  • Do not steal a noun from a nested article. A later de, het or een opens its own noun phrase inside the block. Its noun cannot also be the head governed by the article you started from. In de door de gemeente verstuurde nieuwe brief, gemeente belongs to the inner de; that proves it is not the head of the outer phrase. The inner article does not make the eventual head brief wrong — it only tells you to close the inner noun phrase and keep looking.
  • A participle closes the block. The block ends in a participle, in -e or -en. The head is the first noun after it, not necessarily the next word. If you cannot find a participle anywhere between the article and your candidate, this is not a left-hand modifier and something else is going on.
  • The head agrees with the article you started from. Het … formulier works because formulier is neuter. If your head is a de-word and the phrase opened with het, the head is wrong — chapter 05's test, now doing structural work rather than semantic work.
  • The round trip. Turn the left-hand block into a right-hand relative clause and see whether it survives. het door u ingevulde formulier becomes het formulier dat door u is ingevuld; the two say the same thing. If your version comes out ungrammatical, or means something else, you bracketed the block wrongly.
  • Door marks who did it. Inside one of these blocks, door introduces the agent, not a route or a cause. If your reading has it meaning through, check the bracketing again.

Where this procedure cannot decide

Chapter 05's limit has not gone away, and stacking makes it sharper. The procedure reliably tells you that a noun is modified and where the modifier stops. It does not always tell you which noun.

Two nouns of the same gender and number. If both sit in front of a relative clause, the pointer eliminates neither and no amount of procedure will settle it. You are reading for sense at that point, and so is a Dutch reader.

Two neuter nouns and a left-hand block. Knowing that the article belongs to a noun does not tell you which, and a long block can put several candidates between the article and the participle.

A missing -e removes a signal. The inflection is ordinary adjective agreement, so it is absent after een with a neuter singular: een door u ingevuld formulier is a left-hand block with no -e on it at all. Do not read the missing ending as evidence that this is not the construction — here the test you would most like to use is simply not available.

What the procedure guarantees is smaller than it looks and worth having anyway: you will be choosing between two readings rather than staring at a wall, and you will know that you are choosing.

Step 4 In your own post

This is the chapter with a search you can run mechanically. Go through a letter looking for de or het followed by material that cannot be an ordinary adjective — a preposition, a date, an adverb. Do not flag de nieuwe brief merely because the noun is delayed by one word. Each real hit is a candidate for a left-hand modifier; confirm it by finding the participle and then the first noun after it. The ones that confirm are sentences you have probably been skipping.

Where they cluster is worth knowing. Definitions and conditions use them heavily, because a condition is precisely a noun with a qualification attached, and putting the qualification in front makes the sentence look like a single term. And anything referring to enclosed documents — the form you filled in, the declaration you signed, the decision taken on a date — is the natural home of the construction, which is why the first example in this chapter is the shape it is.

The contrast between your own letters is worth noticing too. A modern Belastingdienst letter largely avoids these; an older housing or immigration document is full of them. Same language, same decade, different institutional habits — and knowing which kind of document you are holding tells you which reading problems to expect.

Run the same five tests from step 3 on every candidate the search turns up, and run the first one before you do anything else. The nested-article test stops you taking a noun from inside the block as the outer head; the participle test tells you where that block closes. Neither test says the very next word must be the head.

Then say plainly which of your hits you could not finish. Three shapes are worth marking separately: a block with no participle in it — which means this was probably not the construction at all; a head noun that disagrees with the article that opened the phrase — which means the head is wrong and the sentence is still closed; and a relative clause with more than one grammatically possible antecedent, where you have picked one for sense. That last pile is not a failure of your reading. It is a property of the sentence, and a Dutch reader has the same problem — the difference is that they will notice, and the point of this chapter is that now you will too.

References

Donaldson covers relative pronouns at printed pages 63 and 65–68. Three sections back this chapter specifically: §8.5.6 (printed 65) for waarvan and van wie; §8.5.8 (printed 66) for wat as a relative; and §8.5.9 (printed 67), which records that welk(e) often replaces die in formal writing.

The left-hand construction is §8.5.10, also printed 67, under the heading adjectival adjuncts replacing relative clauses. The framing this chapter is built on — that the two are one device — is his: he presents them as paired alternatives, with the same content on either side of the noun, and observes that the adjunct always contains a participle. The pairs printed above were written for this book; the technique of pairing them is his.

Neither Taaltalent nor De Opmaat reaches the left-hand construction at this level, and neither teaches welke. That is not a criticism of either: both are building toward production, and a learner does not need to write these. A reader meets them in the first formal letter that arrives.

Kaart

Nieuw

  • Relative clauses stack, each keeping its own verb at the end
  • wie after a preposition for people; waar- + preposition for things
  • welke as a dated formal die
  • wat — and sometimes dat — after alles, iets, niets; wat after a whole clause
  • The left-hand modifier: a compressed clause between the article and its noun — common for completed actions, not available for every relative clause
  • The stronger signal: an article followed by a preposition, date or adverb, rather than merely by an ordinary adjective
  • The head is the first noun after the participle, not necessarily the next word and not the first noun you meet
  • Attributive participles inflect like adjectives: -e after de/het, nothing after een + neuter; strong ones in -en add nothing
  • door marks who did it
  • English allows a bare participle in front, never an agent phrase

Terug

  • What does every modifier attached to a noun contain, whichever side of the noun it sits on? (ch. 05)

    A hole where that noun belongs. That is the gap test, and it is the reason the left-hand block and the relative clause are one device rather than two.

  • What two properties of a noun decide between die and dat? (ch. 05)

    Gender and number — and number wins: every plural takes die regardless of gender, which is why a plural antecedent makes the pointer uninformative.

  • Where does a stacked relative clause put its verb, and what may sit after it? (ch. 02)

    At its own end. Heavy clauses get pushed out past the main verb — the tail rule — which is exactly where a long relative clause tends to land.

  • An attributive participle carries an -e. How do you get back to the dictionary verb underneath it? (ch. 04)

    The same three passes, unchanged: what job the form is doing, where the ge- is, and whether the ending reverses. The -e comes off first and tells you nothing about the verb.
