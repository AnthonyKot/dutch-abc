Part II · What refers to what — Chapter 06

Modifiers on both sides

A clause that describes a noun normally comes after it. Formal Dutch can pick the whole clause up and set it down in front — inside the noun phrase, between the article and the noun. It looks like a different construction. It is the same one, reversed.

The device A noun can be modified from the right by a relative clause, and from the left by a block wedged between its article and itself. Both say the same kind of thing about the noun, both contain a hole where the noun belongs, and formal Dutch chooses between them freely. Recognising that they are one device is most of what makes the left-hand version readable.

Step 1 On the page

Two sentences from the dense part of a letter. The first modifies one noun from both directions at once.

In the style of an official letter

Het door u ingevulde formulier dat wij op 3 juni ontvingen, bevat een fout.

De inspecteur die uw bezwaar behandelt, heeft de gegevens die u ons stuurde nog niet ontvangen.

The second sentence is long but nothing in it is strange: two relative clauses, each sitting behind its own noun, each sending its verb to the end of its own stretch of text exactly as chapter 02 said it would. You can read it slowly and get there.

The first sentence is where reading stops, and it stops at the second word. After het you expect a noun. What arrives is door, which is a preposition, so the natural move is to start reading a prepositional phrase — and then u arrives, and the phrase ends, and nothing has been said yet. Four words in, an experienced reader of English has no structure to hang anything on.

The sentence is not difficult. Het … formulier is a perfectly ordinary noun phrase, and door u ingevulde has been inserted into the middle of it. Once you know that is a thing Dutch does, the sentence takes about as long to read as the second one.

Step 2 What it is doing

First, the ordinary side

Chapter 05 gave you the machinery already: a relative clause follows its noun, opens with a pointer chosen to match that noun's gender and number, and contains a gap where the noun belongs. Three additions are all this chapter needs on that side.

They stack, and each keeps its own verb at the end. The second sentence in step 1 has two of them, and the only thing that makes it long is that each relative clause obeys chapter 02 independently. Find the end of each clause and you have found each verb.

When the pointer needs a preposition, the form changes. For a person, the preposition simply comes first with wie:

de persoon aan wie u de sleutel geeft

For a thing, Dutch does not say aan die. It welds the preposition onto waar-:

de brief waarin wij de termijn noemen

That is waar plus in, and it means in which. There is a whole family of these — waarvan, waarbij, waarop — and they can come apart and sit at opposite ends of a clause. All of that is chapter 07. Here you need only to recognise a waar- word as a pointer with a preposition attached, and to know that the preposition is doing ordinary prepositional work.

And formal writing has its own relative pronoun. Where a course teaches die and dat, official Dutch often writes welke:

de documenten welke u moet meesturen

Donaldson records this directly: welk(e) often replaces die in formal writing. It carries no extra meaning. It is a register marker, and the only thing a reader has to do with it is not be stopped by it. There is also wat, which is the pointer used after alles, iets, niets and after a whole preceding clause, rather than after an ordinary noun:

alles wat u voor 1 juni aan ons stuurt

Now the mirror

Here is the move the chapter exists for. Everything a relative clause says can instead be packed in front of the noun, between it and its article. Donaldson treats these as adjectival adjuncts that replace relative clauses, and the honest way to learn them is in pairs, because the two members say the same thing:

Modifier on the rightThe same thing on the left
het formulier dat u heeft ingevuld het door u ingevulde formulier
de brief die wij u in maart stuurden de in maart aan u gestuurde brief
de beslissing die de inspecteur op 3 juni heeft genomen de op 3 juni door de inspecteur genomen beslissing

Read the right-hand column on its own and it looks like a foreign construction. Read it across from the left-hand column and it is obviously the same sentence with the furniture moved. The article and the noun still belong together — het … formulier, de … brief, de … beslissing — and everything between them is the modifier.

The gap test from chapter 05 still works, and it is the reason to trust the pairing. In het door u ingevulde formulier the block is door u ___ ingevulde: filled in by you, but filled in what? There is a hole, and the noun on the right of the block fills it. That is precisely what a relative clause does; the only difference is which side of the noun the hole sits on.

One word in these blocks deserves naming now, because it recurs. Door marks who performed the action — door u, door de inspecteur. That is the same signal chapter 09 is built on, and you will meet it constantly before then. Here you only need to read it, not analyse it.

What English can and cannot do

It is tempting to say English cannot do this at all. That is not true and the true version is more useful. English puts participles in front of nouns constantly — the completed form, the enclosed documents, a signed declaration. The structure is familiar.

What English refuses

the by-you-filled-in form

Not a sentence anyone would write. English will not carry an agent phrase in front of the noun.

What English does instead

the form filled in by you

The moment there is a by, English sends the whole thing to the right of the noun. Dutch does not have to.

So the difference is narrow and specific: Dutch can put a long modifier, including its agent and its time and place, in front of the noun, and English can only put a short bare participle there. That single fact accounts for nearly all the difficulty, and it is why the blocks that stop you are always the ones with several words in them.

Two spelling details you need

The participle in one of these blocks takes an -e, exactly as an adjective would: ingevuld becomes ingevulde, gestuurd becomes gestuurde. Chapter 04's three passes still recover the verb; the -e comes off first and changes nothing else.

Participles already ending in -en — the strong ones — do not add anything: de op 3 juni genomen beslissing, de ontvangen brief. If a block ends in -en rather than -e, that is chapter 04's signal that you are looking at a strong participle, not evidence that something is missing.

How long these get

Donaldson notes that these adjuncts can reach unwieldy lengths, and quotes a newspaper sentence in which the block runs to more than a dozen words before the noun finally arrives. Official correspondence is milder than journalism, but the principle is the same: there is no grammatical limit, so the block ends when the writer decides it ends. This is the one construction in this book where the honest advice is that the reading procedure matters more than any amount of vocabulary.

Step 3 Read it

The procedure

Three moves, and the first is the one that does the work.

  1. When an article is not followed by a noun, stop reading forwards. Het followed by door, de followed by in or a date — that mismatch is the signal, and it is the only signal you need.
  2. Jump ahead to the first noun. That noun is the head, and it belongs to the article you started from. Now you know what the phrase is about.
  3. Read the block in between as a relative clause, with the head filling its gap.
Reading forwards

Het door u …

Door is a preposition, so a phrase must be starting. It ends at u and has said nothing. Four words in, with no structure — and the temptation is to conclude the sentence is beyond you.

Jumping to the noun

Het … formulier ← door u ingevulde

The form. Which form? The one you filled in. Two pieces, in the order that makes them intelligible rather than the order they were printed in.

Now the whole of step 1's first sentence. The head is formulier, modified on the left by door u ingevulde and on the right by dat wij op 3 juni ontvingen. Both blocks describe the same noun, both have a gap in them that the noun fills, and the sentence's actual claim — bevat een fout — is four words long and arrives last. The form you filled in, which we received on 3 June, contains an error.

Three to try

  1. De door de gemeente verstuurde brief bevat een termijn die u niet mag overschrijden.
  2. Alles wat u voor 1 juni aan ons stuurt, nemen wij mee in de beoordeling.
  3. De persoon aan wie u de sleutel geeft, moet het formulier ondertekenen dat wij hebben meegestuurd.

One. One noun modified from each side, but they are different nouns. De is followed by door, so move one: jump ahead to brief, which is the head. The block is door de gemeente verstuurde — sent by the council. Then the sentence proper begins, and een termijn picks up its own modifier on the right, die u niet mag overschrijden, with die correct because termijn is a de-word. Left block, right clause, one sentence.

Two. Wat, not dat, because the thing being modified is alles rather than an ordinary noun. The gap test confirms it is a pointer and not a conjunction: u voor 1 juni aan ons ___ stuurt is missing its object. Note also that the whole modified phrase is sitting in first position, which is why the main clause opens with its verb — chapter 02's inversion, doing exactly what it always does.

Three. Aan wie, because the head is a person. Then the main clause, and its relative clause has been pushed out past the verb: het formulier ondertekenen dat wij hebben meegestuurd. That is chapter 02's tail again, and it is common precisely here — a heavy relative clause is exactly the sort of thing Dutch prefers to move to the end rather than leave in the middle. The pointer is dat, so it reaches back past ondertekenen to het formulier, the only neuter singular available.

Where it stays ambiguous

Chapter 05's limit has not gone away and stacking makes it sharper. If two nouns of the same gender and number both sit in front of a relative clause, the pointer eliminates neither, and no amount of procedure will settle it — you are reading for sense at that point. The same is true of a left-hand block: if a sentence contains two neuter nouns, knowing that the article belongs to a noun does not tell you which. What the procedure guarantees is that you will be choosing between two readings rather than staring at a wall.

Step 4 In your own post

This is the chapter with a search you can run mechanically. Go through a letter looking only for de or het followed by something that is not a noun — a preposition, a date, an adverb. Every hit is a left-hand modifier, and each one is a sentence you have probably been skipping.

Where they cluster is worth knowing. Definitions and conditions use them heavily, because a condition is precisely a noun with a qualification attached, and putting the qualification in front makes the sentence look like a single term. And anything referring to enclosed documents — the form you filled in, the declaration you signed, the decision taken on a date — is the natural home of the construction, which is why the first example in this chapter is the shape it is.

The contrast between your own letters is worth noticing too. A modern Belastingdienst letter largely avoids these; an older housing or immigration document is full of them. Same language, same decade, different institutional habits — and knowing which kind of document you are holding tells you which reading problems to expect.

References

Donaldson covers relative pronouns at printed pages 63 and 65–68. Three sections back this chapter specifically: §8.5.6 (printed 65) for waarvan and van wie; §8.5.8 (printed 66) for wat as a relative; and §8.5.9 (printed 67), which records that welk(e) often replaces die in formal writing.

The left-hand construction is §8.5.10, also printed 67, under the heading adjectival adjuncts replacing relative clauses. The framing this chapter is built on — that the two are one device — is his: he presents them as paired alternatives, with the same content on either side of the noun, and observes that the adjunct always contains a participle. The pairs printed above were written for this book; the technique of pairing them is his.

Neither Taaltalent nor De Opmaat reaches the left-hand construction at this level, and neither teaches welke. That is not a criticism of either: both are building toward production, and a learner does not need to write these. A reader meets them in the first formal letter that arrives.
