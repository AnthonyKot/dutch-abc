About this book

What it covers, what it refuses to cover, where its claims come from, and how the Dutch examples are checked.

Draft status The complete fourteen-chapter map is settled. Chapters 01–13 are written; the rest are in progress.

Who it is for

An adult who has lived in the Netherlands for years, knows the basics, and cannot comfortably read the post. Not a beginner: someone with a great deal of exposure and no reliable way through it. The workarounds — a colleague, a translator, filing it unread — function well enough that nothing forces the issue, which is why the situation persists.

The book assumes you can read Dutch words aloud and recognise common ones, roughly the vocabulary of a finished A1 course. It assumes no grammar. It does not assume you were paying attention in the course you abandoned.

It also assumes you do not need to be protected from an explanation. The cartoon-mnemonic register of much beginner material is not a kindness to an adult with an analytical job; it is part of why the rules were never plainly stated.

What it covers, and what it does not

Reading only. This book does not teach listening, speaking or writing, and it is not preparation for the inburgeringsexamen.

That is a deliberate scope, not an omission to apologise for. Reading real documents is a distinct skill with distinct obstacles, and it is the one this format can actually teach — the artefacts are text, and they can sit on the page next to the explanation. A static, text-only book cannot adequately teach listening, and one that claimed to would be selling you something.

Within reading, the target is everyday life in the Netherlands rather than an exam syllabus: letters, forms, contracts, statements, signage, messages. Chapter 14, which is not yet written, will work through a reconstructed Belastingdienst letter — to be composed for this book and modelled on a real one. No real letter will be reproduced.

How it is built

Twelve teaching chapters introduce the devices; two final chapters show how they work together in complete documents. The chapters run in dependency order — none uses something a later chapter introduces. Each teaching chapter follows four steps:

  1. On the page — a real fragment, and a plain statement of where the eye stops.
  2. What it is doing — the device, with a rule you can apply.
  3. Read it — you work it, including the wrong parse you will reach for first.
  4. In your own post — where it appears in documents you already have.

Each chapter opens with a single question about the chapter before it, asked before anything is restated. Reading a summary is not the same as recalling it, and the difference is the whole reason the question comes first.

The worked answers in step 3 are hidden until you open them. That is deliberate and it is the one piece of friction this book insists on: once you have seen an answer you can no longer find out whether you would have reached it, and that is the only fact about your own reading worth having. Write your answer down, then open the box.

Every chapter then gives you self-check criteria — tests you can run on a letter nobody has an answer key for, because that is the situation you are actually in. Most of them can only tell you that you are wrong, which is still the thing worth knowing. And every chapter names one case its procedure cannot settle. A reading method that never admits a limit teaches you to force an answer, and forcing an answer out of an official letter is worse than knowing you have not got one.

Then a kaart: what this chapter introduced, which earlier chapters it built on, and what it deliberately postponed. The terug column is a set of questions rather than a summary, for the same reason as the opening prompt. The postponements are visible so that a chapter cannot quietly depend on something you have not been given.

Sources

Specific claims about Dutch are checked against the reference works below. Where a claim cannot be checked, it is either cut or marked as unverified.

  • Bruce C. Donaldson, Dutch Reference Grammar (Martinus Nijhoff, 1981) — the only one of these that explains rather than drills. It is also forty-five years old and, by its author's own description, strongly prescriptive. It is treated as authoritative for structure and for what a form is, and not for current usage or social register.
  • Beersmans & Tersteeg, De Opmaat: naar NT2-niveau A2 (2009).
  • Verbruggen & Taks, Taaltalent deel 1 and 2 (Coutinho, 2020) — the A1 and A1→A2 volumes.

Where the 1981 grammar disagrees with the modern course books about how people write now, the modern books win and the chapter says the older account differs.

Encyclopaedias, forums, blogs and language apps are used to work out what to look up. They are never cited. No source text is reproduced anywhere on this site: the content of a rule is free, an author's sentences are not. Every Dutch example here was written for this book or observed in the wild.

Why several chapters exist

Several chapters cover material that beginner courses omit. Some of it appears in reference grammars but is not presented as a practical reading technique for someone at this level; some of it is simply not there. Both kinds were checked rather than assumed:

  • Not treated as a reading technique anywhere. Decomposing an unfamiliar compound (chapter 01), and reading official Dutch as a genre with a predictable shape (chapter 13).
  • In the reference grammar, but not in either course at this level. The daarbij / hiervan / waarop family — Donaldson gives it a chapter of its own, while De Opmaat covers three uses of er and Taaltalent 2 covers one, none of them these (chapter 07).
  • Absent from all three. Dienen te as the written form of obligation (chapter 08); tenzij, mits and indien, the conditional joints of a real letter (chapter 10); and the modal particles as a category (chapter 11).

How Dutch examples are mechanically checked

Wrong Dutch in a book about Dutch is worse than a wrong number in a book about mathematics: you cannot detect it, and you will learn it. So every Dutch example on this site is marked up in the HTML, extracted mechanically, and put through a set of checks before publication — article agreement against a gender register, singular and plural, and every compound split against the parts it claims to decompose into. A failure blocks publication rather than raising a warning.

These checks catch a narrow, specific class of error. They do not verify inflection, tense formation, word order or idiom, they cannot tell whether a sentence is natural, and they are not a guarantee of correctness. Nothing replaces the reference works above.

A second pass scans the published files for structured identifiers — personal and account numbers, postcodes, phone numbers, email addresses — and for a private list of names. It matches patterns, not meaning, so it is a safety net under a manual reading rather than a substitute for one. Real post is this book's best material and its only privacy risk.

Reading this book by ear

Every Dutch fragment on this site is tagged as Dutch in the markup, so a screen reader switches voice for it and pronounces it as Dutch rather than as oddly spelled English. There are over twelve hundred of them and the tagging is checked mechanically before publication, on the same footing as the checks above — in a book that mixes two languages in almost every sentence, saying which language a word is in is part of saying what the word is.

Every page also carries a skip link, and the headings form a proper outline with no skipped levels, so the book can be navigated by heading rather than by scrolling. That is the whole of what has been done, and it is not an accessibility audit. Colour contrast, focus order and reading order have not been formally assessed. If you read this book with assistive technology and something obstructs you, that is a defect worth reporting rather than a limit of the format.

The series

Fourth in a series of static books built the same way — plain HTML, no build step, one stylesheet — after The Quantum Quartet (four physicists on early quantum mechanics), The Bridge (a quantum mechanics course carried forward into quantum information) and No Such Form (the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, one rung at a time).
