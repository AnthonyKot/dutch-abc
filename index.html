Reading real Dutch · A1 → A2

DutchABC

This is not your first Dutch course. It is for someone who has lived in the Netherlands for years, knows the basics, and still has a stack of Dutch post they cannot comfortably read.

Draft status The complete chapter map below is settled. Chapters are now being written.

Most beginner books assume Dutch is new to you. Here it is already everywhere: on the mat, on the screen, on the form at the counter. You have learned to work around it — ask a colleague, use a translator, file it unread and hope. The workarounds function well enough that years can pass without the underlying problem being solved.

Vocabulary matters, but it is not the whole problem. Real Dutch uses a small set of recurring devices that can make familiar words and sentences hard to recognise. Nouns are welded into compounds you will not find as entries. Verbs split in half and the halves sit ten or fifteen words apart. Pronouns fuse with prepositions into daarbij, hiervan, waarop. Official Dutch removes the actor from its sentences and puts its one actionable line in the fourth paragraph.

Each of these is mechanical rather than mysterious, and the core mechanism of each can be explained in a page. Courses teach some of them as grammar, but rarely assemble them into a practical method for reading real documents — because a course teaches you to read the course, and the course was written to be readable. This book is about the Dutch that was not written for you.

Twelve teaching chapters introduce the devices. Two final chapters show how they work together in complete documents. Each teaching chapter follows four steps: On the page (a real fragment you cannot currently get through), What it is doing (the device, with a rule you can apply), Read it (you work it, including the wrong parse you will reach for first), and In your own post (where it appears in documents you already have). Then a kaart: what was new, what it built on, and what is deliberately postponed.

The last chapter reads a Belastingdienst letter end to end, using every device in the book and introducing nothing new.

Scope. This book covers reading only. It does not teach listening, speaking or writing, and it does not prepare you for the inburgeringsexamen. Those are different skills with different methods, and pretending otherwise would waste your time. About says what is covered and how each claim was checked.

Part I · What makes a known word unrecognisable

Three devices that hide vocabulary you already have. Between them they account for a great many of the moments where an otherwise readable sentence stops you.

Before you begin You can read Dutch words aloud and recognise common ones on sight — roughly the vocabulary of a finished A1 course. No grammar is assumed. Everything else is built here.

  • 01Words you cannot look up Covers: compounds, decomposed right to left — arbeidsongeschiktheidsverzekering and its kind
  • 02The verb at the end Covers: the verb bracket — why the word that carries the meaning arrives last, and why a reader should jump to it first
  • 03Verbs that split Covers: separable verbs at a distance — bel … op, where you looked up both halves and both were wrong

Part II · What refers to what

Once a sentence is long, the question stops being what the words mean and becomes which one attaches to which. Three devices answer it — and the third, daarbij and its family, is the one that beginner courses do not reach at this level.

Builds on Part I. Chapter 05 combines ideas introduced in chapters 02 and 04, and is the first chapter that combines rather than introduces.

  • 04de, het, die, dat Covers: gender as the tag that tells you which noun a later clause points back to — a reader's problem, not a speaker's embarrassment
  • 05The clause that hangs off Covers: relative clauses, stacked, each with its own verb at the end — a very common shape in an official sentence
  • 06Daarbij, hiervan, waarop Covers: the er / hier- / daar- / waar- family — one closed system, a preposition and a pointer written backwards

Part III · Who must do what, and when

The machinery of formal Dutch. All three devices here work by making the obligation harder to locate — a different tense from the one you were taught first, a politer verb than the one you know, and a grammar that removes whoever has to act.

Builds on Parts I and II. This is the densest stretch of the book; one document stays visible throughout it.

  • 07The other past tense Covers: the imperfect — courses teach the perfect first because speech uses it; written Dutch leans on the other one
  • 08Moeten, dienen, hoeven Covers: obligation in its written register — how moet becomes dient u te
  • 09Nobody does anything Covers: the passive and nominalisation, and how to restore the actor they removed — who files, who decides, who pays

Part IV · Register

Two chapters on recognising which kind of Dutch you are holding, and what its markers mean. Getting a single connective wrong here can invert the meaning of a letter.

Builds on Parts I–III. Chapter 10's connectives govern clauses built in chapters 02 and 05.

  • 10Echter, tenzij, mits Covers: the joints of a formal text — the words carrying the conditions and the exceptions, easy to read past until you know them
  • 11U, je, and the buurtapp Covers: the two written registers side by side — Geachte heer/mevrouw against a message full of diminutives and particles
