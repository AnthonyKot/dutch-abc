About this book

What it covers, what it refuses to cover, where every claim came from, and how the Dutch on these pages is checked.

Who it is for

An adult who has lived in the Netherlands for years, is somewhere around A1, and cannot read the post. Not a beginner: someone with a great deal of exposure and no way through it. The workarounds — a colleague, a translator, filing it unread — work well enough that nothing forces the issue, which is exactly why the situation persists.

The book assumes you can read Dutch words aloud and recognise common ones, roughly the vocabulary of a finished A1 course. It assumes no grammar. It does not assume you were paying attention in the course you abandoned.

It also assumes you do not need to be protected from an explanation. The cartoon-mnemonic register of most beginner material is not a kindness to an adult with an analytical job; it is part of why the rules were never actually stated.

What it covers, and what it does not

Reading only. This book does not teach listening, speaking or writing, and it is not preparation for the inburgeringsexamen.

That is a deliberate scope, not an omission to be apologised for. Reading real documents is a distinct skill with distinct obstacles, and it is the one a static book can actually teach — the artefacts are text, and they can sit on the page next to the explanation. Listening cannot be taught this way, and a book that claimed to would be selling you something.

Within reading, the target is everyday life in the Netherlands rather than an exam syllabus: letters, forms, contracts, statements, signage, messages. Chapter 14's test is whether you can read a letter that actually arrived.

How it is built

Fourteen chapters, one device each, in dependency order — no chapter uses something a later chapter introduces. Each follows four steps:

  1. On the page — a real fragment, and a plain statement of where the eye stops.
  2. What it is doing — the device, with a rule you can apply.
  3. Read it — you work it, including the wrong parse you will reach for first.
  4. In your own post — where it appears in documents you already have.

Then a kaart: what this chapter introduced, which earlier chapters it leaned on, and what it deliberately postponed. The postponements are visible so that a chapter cannot quietly depend on something you have not been given.

Sources

Every specific claim about Dutch is checked against a copy of a real book. Where a claim cannot be checked, it is either cut or marked. The reference shelf is:

  • Bruce C. Donaldson, Dutch Reference Grammar (Martinus Nijhoff, 1981) — the only one of these that explains rather than drills. It is also forty-five years old and, by its author's own description, strongly prescriptive. It is treated as authoritative for structure and for what a form is, and not for current usage or social register.
  • Beersmans & Tersteeg, De Opmaat: naar NT2-niveau A2 (2009).
  • Verbruggen & Taks, Taaltalent deel 1 and 2 (Coutinho, 2020) — the A1 and A1→A2 volumes.

Where the 1981 grammar disagrees with the modern course books about how people write now, the modern books win and the chapter says the older account differs.

Encyclopaedias, forums, blogs and language apps are used to work out what to look up. They are never cited. No source text is reproduced anywhere on this site: the content of a rule is free, an author's sentences are not. Every Dutch example here was written for this book or observed in the wild.

What the courses do not cover

Several chapters exist because a device is common in real documents and absent from the teaching material. That is checked rather than assumed — the following are absent from De Opmaat's grammar reference, from Taaltalent 1 and 2, and from Donaldson's 276 pages, in the sense that no source treats them as something a reader must be taught:

  • decomposing an unfamiliar compound as a reading technique (chapter 01);
  • the daarbij / hiervan / waarop forms at this level — Donaldson has the chapter, neither course reaches it (chapter 06);
  • dienen te as the written form of obligation (chapter 08);
  • tenzij, mits and indien — the conditional joints of a real letter (chapter 10);
  • the modal particles as a category (chapter 11);
  • official Dutch as a genre with a predictable shape (chapter 13).

The Dutch on these pages is checked by machine

Wrong Dutch in a book about Dutch is worse than a wrong number in a book about mathematics: you cannot detect it, and you will learn it. So every Dutch example on this site is marked up in the HTML, extracted mechanically, and checked before publication — article agreement against a gender register, adjective inflection, participle and imperfect formation, and every compound split against its claimed parts. A failure blocks publication rather than raising a warning.

The same pass scans any real document text for anything that should not be public: personal numbers, account numbers, addresses, names. Real post is this book's best material and its only privacy risk.
